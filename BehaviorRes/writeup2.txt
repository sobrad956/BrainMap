A from-scratch account of the science, the Brain-Wide Map paper's choices,
and every cut we made afterward
================================================================================

This file is meant to be readable if you have never taken a neuroscience
class, never trained a model, and have never heard of the International
Brain Laboratory or the Brain-Wide Map. Technical steps are still here,
in order, with numbers. What is new relative to writeup.txt is the
argument: why each step is a scientific claim about brains, not just a
data-cleaning recipe.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
0. The one-sentence claim, then the rest of the file
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Claim: if you want to know what motor cortex is doing during the IBL
decision task, you should predict the paw, not the wheel. The wheel is a
convenient one-dimensional object in the world. Motor cortex does not
innervate the wheel. It innervates muscles that move the limb. The limb
sometimes drags the wheel and sometimes does not. Those two situations
are different computations, and treating wheel speed as "the movement"
hides that difference.

The original Brain-Wide Map (BWM) paper used wheel velocity as a movement
target because it was already logged, it is the animal's report of the
decision, and it is the same physical object in every lab. That was a
reasonable proxy for a paper whose main question was "where in the brain
can we read out the choice." It is a bad proxy for a paper whose question
is "how does cortex generate the movement that implements the choice."

Those are not the same question. This dataset is built for the second
one.

The rest of this file, in order:

    1.  What a brain recording even is: the probe, the raw voltage,
        spike sorting, isolation QC, other signals besides spikes,
        and how a channel is mapped onto a named brain area
    2.  What the mouse is asked to do
    3.  What the BWM paper asked, and why the wheel was their movement
    4.  Why neural activity should not be thought of as encoding the wheel
    5.  Why the paw is the right object instead
    6.  Why this task is not a ballistic reach, and why that matters
    7.  Still / coupled / decoupled as motor primitives
    8.  Why those primitives should be encoded differently
    9.  What we kept from the paper on purpose
    10. The pipeline, step by step, with the scientific reason for each cut
        (including right-camera ×2, 2D speed, and speedNormalized)
    11. What a ModelDataLeft / ModelDataRight trial contains
    12. Hemisphere, laterality, and the literature
    13. Hemisphere-pure copies: LeftIpsi and RightContra
    14. Authors versus us, in one place
    15. Honest limitations
    16. Counts, ancestry, scripts

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
1. What we are looking at when we look at a "unit"
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

A neuron is a cell that communicates by brief electrical pulses called
spikes (also called action potentials). A spike is approximately a one-
millisecond event. It is all-or-none: the cell either fires or it does
not. The information a neuron can send to the rest of the brain is
carried in the times of those spikes, not in their height.

The rest of this section is what that sentence hides: the silicon
(1.1), the raw voltage and the features we did not keep (1.2), the
sorter the paper actually ran (1.3), the three isolation tests (1.4),
and the atlas that turns a contact into MOp5 (1.5). After sorting, a
"unit" is one putative neuron: a list of timestamps at which that cell
fired. It is not the only thing the electrode measured.

Not every sorted blob is a clean neuron. Spike sorting is an algorithm,
not a microscope: it groups similar voltage waveforms and calls each
group a "unit." Some groups really are one cell. Some are two cells
glued together (contamination: extra spikes that do not belong). Some
are one cell with half its spikes missing (false negatives: the
algorithm only caught the big spikes). Some are just electrical noise.
If you decode movement from a contaminated unit, you are mixing two
cells' opinions. If you decode from a truncated unit, you are looking
at a random subset of the cell's spikes and calling the gaps "silence."
That is why isolation quality control exists.

Before those tests make sense, you need the hardware, the raw voltage,
the sorter the paper actually ran, and the atlas that turns a contact
on a silicon needle into a name like MOp5. Those are not garnish.
They are the generative model of the numbers in spike_counts. A
decoder that treats every unit as an independent Bernoulli draw is
ignoring the fact that two units 20 micrometres apart on the same
shank saw overlapping extracellular fields, that the LFP on those
channels is a different physical quantity from the spike times, and
that "MOp5" is a voxel in a mouse-specific atlas after a human-plus-
algorithm alignment, not a GPS coordinate of the cell body.


1.1  The Neuropixels probe: 960 electrodes, 384 live channels, and
     a checkerboard that is the reason sorting works

The Brain-Wide Map recordings in this freeze were made with Neuropixels
1.0 probes (Jun, Steinmetz, Siegle, Harris, Carandini, and colleagues,
Nature 2017; IMEC commercial device). A Neuropixels 1.0 is a single
straight silicon shank, about 10 millimetres long, with a cross-section
of roughly 70 by 20–24 micrometres. That is thinner than a human hair
and long enough, in a mouse, to go from the cortical surface through
motor cortex, striatum, and into thalamus on one insertion. The same
physical object in a macaque or a human would sample a much smaller
fraction of the brain; the geometry is absolute, the coverage is not.

Along that shank sit 960 electrode sites. Each site is a 12 by 12
micrometre square of titanium nitride, impedance on the order of 150
kiloohms at 1 kHz. They are not 960 independent wires. They are metal
pads fabricated on one CMOS chip, switchable onto a smaller number of
recording channels.

The distinction that matters:

    electrode / site     a physical contact on the shank (960 of them)
    channel              a live amplifier + ADC that can listen to one
                         electrode at a time (384 of them)
    bank / channel map   which 384 of the 960 are connected right now
    unit                 a putative neuron, inferred later from the
                         384 live voltage traces

You cannot record all 960 sites at once. The 384 figure is a hardware
ceiling: there are 384 parallel, dual-band recording channels in the
probe base. CMOS switches under the sites let the experimenter pick
which 384 electrodes those channels address. IBL's default, and the
map used for essentially all BWM insertions, is the dense contiguous
layout: the most distal (tip-ward) 384 sites, forming a gap-free
column about 3.84 millimetres tall. That choice is not cosmetic.
Kilosort isolates neurons by matching a spike's spatial footprint
across neighboring sites. If you recorded a sparse, gappy subset of
electrodes, that footprint would be undersampled and sorting would
get worse. The unused 576 sites are still on the shank; they are just
not wired to an ADC during the session. A later session in the same
animal could, in principle, switch to a more dorsal bank and listen
to a different 3.84 mm of the same track. The BWM protocol did not
do that mid-session. One insertion, one bank, one 3.84 mm column.

How the 384 live sites are arranged (IBL `neuropixel.dense_layout`
version=1, which matches the IMEC 1.0 checkerboard):

    four columns across the shank
    two sites per row
    20 micrometres of vertical (axial) pitch from row to row
    16 micrometres of lateral pitch from column to column
    columns occupied in a repeating stagger 2, 0, 3, 1 so that
        nearest neighbors form a checkerboard, not a square grid
    12 by 12 micrometre pads, so the metal almost tiles the pitch
    192 rows × 20 µm = 3.84 mm of recorded depth

The original Jun et al. paper quotes 25 micrometres as the centre-to-
centre nearest-neighbor distance, which is the diagonal of that
checkerboard, not a contradiction of IBL's 16/20 numbers. IBL's
geometry header stores, per live channel: x, y (lateral and axial
on the shank, micrometres), row, col, shank (always 0 for NP 1.0),
ADC index, and sample_shift.

sample_shift is a modelling landmine if you ever go back to raw
traces. The 384 channels are not sampled at the same instant.
Neuropixels 1.0 has 32 analogue-to-digital converters; each ADC
round-robins 12 channels inside one 30 kHz sample period (~33
microseconds). Channels on different ADCs therefore have systematic
sub-sample time offsets, stored as sample_shift. Spike-sorting
interpolates them. A naive "the voltage on channel 17 at sample t
is simultaneous with channel 203" is false at the 10-microsecond
scale. For 20 ms binned spike counts this does not matter. For
waveform alignment, spike-LFP phase, or any claim about millisecond
synchrony across the probe, it does.

What a modeller can actually use from this geometry, even without
reopening the raw files:

    axial_um     depth of the unit's peak channel along the shank,
                 tip = 0. Two units 20 µm apart are neighbors.
                 Two units 2000 µm apart are in different structures
                 even if they share a probe_name.
    lateral_um   which of the four columns. Almost never used in
                 decoding papers; it is the only handle on the
                 shank's medio-lateral micro-position.
    channel      integer 0–383 on THAT probe. Channel 40 on probe00
                 is not channel 40 on probe01. After we concatenate
                 probes, unit_idx is the modelling index; channel
                 is not unique in a session.
    probe_name   probe00 or probe01. Many BWM sessions have two
                 shanks in the same animal at once, in different
                 structures. Our spike_counts columns from two
                 probes are two independent 3.84 mm worlds that
                 happen to share a clock.
    pid          the insertion UUID. The identity of "this piece of
                 silicon in this brain on this day."

Spatial facts that leak into any model of these spike trains,
whether you model them or not:

    One neuron is seen on several sites. Extracellular amplitude
    falls off over tens of micrometres. A well-isolated cell on
    this geometry typically has a spatial footprint of ~5–20
    channels. That is why dense silicon works and a single
    microwire does not: the sorter is clustering a spatiotemporal
    movie, not a 1-D voltage wiggle.

    Nearby units are not independent observations of the brain.
    They are nearby in tissue, they share local inhibition, they
    share the same LFP, and they were competing for the same
    templates during sorting. Treating 200 motor units as i.i.d.
    features overstates the number of independent samples.

    The recorded column is a 1-D (really a very thin 2-D) sample
    of a 3-D volume. Motor cortex is a sheet. A shank through MOp
    gives you a stack of layers (2/3, 5, 6a, 6b) at one (AP, ML)
    location, not a map of the motor sheet. You cannot reconstruct
    a somatotopic map from one insertion. You CAN ask whether
    layer 5 units predict paw speed differently from layer 2/3,
    because the shank actually stacks those layers.

    Dead, noisy, and out-of-brain channels exist. IBL detects them
    (outside the brain: flat traces; dead: tiny amplitude; noisy:
    excess variance) and interpolates or excludes them before
    sorting. A 384-channel file does not mean 384 working ears.

    The shank displaces tissue, dimples the surface, and leaves
    gliosis. Histology is partly a correction for the fact that
    the brain was not in the atlas pose while the probe was in it.

    Neuropixels 2.0 (two-column linear sites, or four shanks) is
    a different geometry. This freeze is 1.0. Do not mix the
    pitch numbers.

The 10 mm shank vs the 3.84 mm live window also explains coverage
quirks in our folders. An insertion aimed at left MOp may have its
live bank sitting in cortex plus striatum, or in cortex plus
thalamus, depending on how deep the tip went and which bank was
on. Units from the same probe_name in the same session can be in
MOp5 and in a completely different region a millimetre deeper.
brain_area is per unit, not per probe.


1.2  What the electrode actually records, and why spikes are not
     the only derived feature

The physical measurement is extracellular voltage versus time, at
each live site, relative to a reference (on NP 1.0, typically a
large tip contact and/or an external reference). That voltage is a
sum of everything nearby that moves charge: the action potentials
of cells within ~50–100 µm, the slower synaptic currents of a much
larger neighborhood, volume-conducted fields from farther away,
muscle artifact, and electronic noise.

Neuropixels 1.0 splits this on-chip into two streams at once
(dual-band):

    AP  ("action potential" / spike band)
        analogue filter roughly 0.3–10 kHz
        sampled at 30,000 samples per second
        stored as the `.ap.bin` file
        this is what spike sorting eats

    LFP ("local field potential")
        analogue filter roughly 0.5–500 Hz
        sampled at 2,500 samples per second
        stored as the `.lf.bin` file
        this is NOT sent into Kilosort

A spike, in this picture, is a ~1 millisecond deflection of tens
to hundreds of microvolts on a handful of neighboring AP-band
channels. The sorter's job (next subsection) is to turn those
deflections into event times labelled by putative neuron. After
that, the modelling literature — and this dataset — almost always
throws away the voltage and keeps the event times. That is a
choice, not a law of nature. The same electrode, the same 384
channels, the same session, contain several other derived features
that have been shown, repeatedly, to carry movement information.

What you can derive, and why a modeller might care:

    1. Sorted spike times (what we use).
       Single-unit activity (SUA). One putative cell, 0/1 events.
       Finest spatial grain, highest interpretability, most
       fragile to isolation errors and to unit dropout over time.

    2. The spike waveform itself, not just the time.
       Each unit has a template: a short spatiotemporal snippet
       (typically ~1–2 ms × ~10–20 channels). From that you can
       compute trough-to-peak duration, peak-to-trough ratio,
       spatial spread, and amplitude. Putative fast-spiking
       (narrow-waveform, often PV interneurons) versus regular-
       spiking (broad-waveform, often excitatory pyramidal)
       cells are distinguished this way in cortex, including
       motor cortex. Those classes do not play the same role in
       generating movement. A decoder that lumps them is mixing
       two circuits. We did not split them. The waveforms are in
       the IBL release; they are not in our spike_counts tensor.

    3. Multi-unit activity (MUA) / threshold crossings / hash.
       Ignore identity. Count every threshold crossing on a
       channel, or take the envelope of the AP-band voltage, or
       sum all unsorted spikes near a site. Trautmann, O'Shea,
       Sun, and colleagues (Neuron 2019, "Accurate Estimation of
       Neural Population Dynamics without Spike Sorting") showed
       that for many population-level questions, including
       decoding, this unsorted hash recovers dynamics similar to
       fully sorted units. The reason is statistical: a decoder
       of a movement is using a population rate, and nearby
       cells are correlated enough that knowing "someone in this
       50 µm neighborhood fired" is already a lot. MUA is also
       more stable when isolation quality drifts. The cost is
       that you can no longer say "this MOp5 pyramidal cell."

    4. Spike-band power.
       The energy in the AP band, per channel, in a 20 ms bin,
       without detecting events. Related to MUA. Robust. Cheap.
       Mixes many cells and some noise.

    5. Local field potential, by frequency band.
       The LFP is dominated by synaptic currents, not by action
       potentials (Buzsáki, Anastassiou, Koch, Nature Reviews
       Neuroscience 2012). It is a mesoscale signal: one channel
       "hears" a few hundred micrometres to millimetres, not
       50 µm. Classic bands, in motor cortex:

           delta     ~1–4 Hz     often movement-related in
                                 primate M1, also arousal
           theta     ~4–8 Hz     more hippocampal; still shows
                                 up in cortex
           mu/alpha  ~8–12 Hz    sensorimotor rhythm; suppressed
                                 during movement
           beta      ~13–30 Hz   the famous motor-cortical
                                 "status quo" rhythm (Engel and
                                 Fries 2010). High during hold /
                                 posture, suppressed at go.
                                 Baker, Lemon and others tied
                                 beta to isometric hold and to
                                 corticospinal synchrony.
           gamma     ~30–80 Hz   often related to local
                                 processing; mixed
           high-gamma / high-frequency LFP
                     ~80–400 Hz  in motor cortex this band is
                                 the best LFP decoder of
                                 kinematics. It is also the band
                                 most contaminated by actual
                                 spikes leaking through the
                                 filter (Ray and Maunsell,
                                 Neuron 2011). So "high-gamma
                                 encodes movement" and "spikes
                                 encode movement" are not fully
                                 independent claims.

    6. Current-source density (CSD).
       The second spatial difference of the LFP along the shank.
       It sharpens laminar sinks and sources (where current
       enters or leaves the tissue). On a 20 µm-pitch probe this
       is almost the point of the geometry. CSD can tell you
       which layer is receiving synaptic input at go-cue versus
       which layer is spiking. We do not compute it.

    7. Spike-LFP coupling.
       The phase of beta (or of any band) at which a given unit
       prefers to fire. A cell that is locked to beta during
       STILL and unlocks during COUPLED is a different object
       from a cell whose rate just goes up. This is one of the
       few ways the still / coupled / decoupled regimes could
       show up as a change in coordination rather than as a
       change in firing rate. We do not compute it.

    8. Amplitude drift of the unit over the session.
       Kilosort tracks this. A cell whose amplitude slowly falls
       is often the probe drifting relative to the tissue, not
       the cell "adapting." If a decoder's weights on that unit
       silently change over a session, you may be looking at
       drift, not learning.

What the literature has actually shown, in motor cortex, about
these non-spike signals:

    LFP can decode movement. Mehring, Rickert, Vaadia, Cardoso
    de Oliveira, Aertsen, Rotter (Nature Neuroscience 2003)
    reconstructed 2-D hand trajectories from LFP in monkey M1.
    That paper is why people stopped treating LFP as "just
    noise below the spike band."

    High-frequency LFP is the useful kinematic band. Zhuang,
    Truccolo, Vargas-Irwin, Donoghue (Journal of Neurophysiology
    2010) decoded 3-D reach and grasp from LFP up to 400 Hz in
    monkey M1 and found 100–200 Hz and 200–400 Hz carried the
    most information — i.e. the bands that overlap residual
    spike energy. Low-frequency LFP was worse for kinematics
    and better for cruder states (move vs rest).

    With large sorted ensembles, spikes usually win, and LFP is
    not independent extra information. Bansal, Truccolo,
    Vargas-Irwin, Donoghue (Journal of Neurophysiology 2012)
    compared SA (spikes), MUA, and multiband LFP on dual 96-
    electrode arrays in MI and PMv during reach-to-grasp.
    Ensembles of >16 units carried more information than
    multiband LFP, and the LFP contribution was largely nested
    inside the spike information rather than complementary.
    Combined signals helped robustness more than peak accuracy.
    Translation to us: with 5–200 motor units we are in the
    regime where spikes should dominate a kinematic decoder.
    That is not a reason to ignore LFP. It is a reason not to
    expect LFP to add a new kinematic axis once you already
    have a good population.

    LFP and unsorted spikes are more stable over months than
    isolated units. Flint, Wright, Scheid, Rubio, Solla,
    Slutzky (Journal of Neural Engineering 2012, 2013) and
    Perge, Zhang, Malik, Homer, Cash, Friehs, Donoghue,
    Hochberg (JNE 2014, human M1) showed that as units
    disappear from chronic arrays, LFP and threshold-crossing
    rates keep decoding. Stavisky, Kao, Nuyujukian, Ryu,
    Shenoy (J Neurophysiol 2015) found high-frequency LFP
    useful for BCI and partly complementary to spikes. IBL
    insertions are acute (one session per craniotomy, probe
    out at the end), so chronic dropout is not our problem.
    Within-session drift still is.

    Beta is a hold / posture / "do not move" signal, not a
    speed signal. Motor cortical beta power is high during
    steady posture and drops around movement onset (event-
    related desynchronization; Pfurtscheller; Baker, Kilner,
    Pinches, Lemon 1999). Engel and Fries (2010) framed beta
    as a status-quo rhythm. For THIS dataset that is almost
    the definition of our STILL regime versus COUPLED /
    DECOUPLED. A still / coupled / decoupled classifier that
    only sees spike counts may be struggling to see a
    phenomenon that is louder in 20 Hz power on the same
    channels. The BWM paper decoded wheel velocity from
    spikes and did not publish this comparison. We have not
    published it either. The LFP files exist on the IBL
    servers; they are not in ModelDataLeft.

    High-gamma is partly spikes in disguise. Ray and Maunsell
    (Neuron 2011) showed that much of 80–200 Hz power tracks
    the spike rate of nearby cells. Using high-gamma and
    sorted spikes as "two modalities" without accounting for
    that double-counts the same action potentials. Using
    beta and sorted spikes is a cleaner contrast: different
    biophysics, different spatial scale, different hypothesised
    role (state / posture vs. moment-to-moment drive).

    Unsorted activity is often enough for population dynamics.
    Trautmann et al. (Neuron 2019) is the citation if someone
    asks why we bothered with Kilosort at all. The answer for
    this project is scientific, not statistical: we want to
    talk about motor-cortex neurons in named layers, with the
    same isolation the BWM paper used, because the claim is
    about that circuit. If the claim were "predict paw speed
    as well as possible from this craniotomy," MUA plus LFP
    plus spikes would be the honest feature set, and Trautmann
    plus Bansal plus Zhuang would be the methods section.

Why this might matter for the still / coupled / decoupled idea
specifically. Sorted spikes in 20 ms bins are a good model of
the output of motor cortex (which cells fired). They are a
worse model of the input and the state (what synaptic drive
arrived, whether the local circuit is in a hold-beta regime,
whether a population is synchronised without a rate change).
Interactive, contact-rich movement is exactly where those
distinctions have been claimed to live: beta during hold,
desynchronisation at go, high-gamma / MUA during the
kinematic burst, low-frequency LFP tracking slower postural
adjustments. A decoder that fails on DECOUPLED from spikes
alone has two interpretations: (1) motor cortex does not
represent unloaded paw motion well, or (2) it does, but not
in the single-unit rate. (2) is untestable in our current
tensors because we never built the other features. That is
a limitation, not a finding.

The raw AP traces also contain things sorting is designed to
throw away: overlapping spikes, axonal bursts, stimulation
artifacts (not an IBL issue), and the shape of each spike as
it changes with the cell's recent history. We do not use
those. Mentioning them is the honest statement that
spike_counts is a lossy compression of the electrode.


1.3  Spike sorting as actually run for this paper: IBL's port of
     Kilosort 2.5, revision 2024-05-06

"Spike sorting" means: take the 384 AP-band traces, find the
brief spatiotemporal events that look like action potentials,
and assign each event to a cluster that is supposed to be one
neuron. It is clustering in a space whose axes are "voltage on
each nearby channel, at each of ~30 samples around the event."
A cluster is a template. A spike is assigned to the template
it matches, after accounting for the fact that the whole probe
slowly drifts through the tissue over hours.

The algorithm at the core is Kilosort 2.5 (Pachitariu, Steinmetz,
and the Cortex Lab / HHMI lineage; MATLAB original). Kilosort
2.5's distinctive move, relative to Kilosort 2, is explicit
drift correction: it estimates a time-varying depth shift of
the recording and registers the data so that a cell that slid
50 µm down the shank over the session is still one cluster,
not two. Without that, a three-hour IBL session would fracture
units. The rest of the engine is template matching: initialise
templates from detected spikes, subtract the explained voltage,
detect leftovers, iterate, merge templates that are really the
same cell.

IBL does not run stock MATLAB Kilosort 2.5 on the raw `.ap.bin`
and call it a day. They documented a pipeline (International
Brain Laboratory, "Spike sorting pipeline for the International
Brain Laboratory," Figshare, doi:10.6084/m9.figshare.19705522,
v4) and implemented it as a Python port whose clustering step
is Kilosort 2.5 (the `ibl-sorter` / earlier `pykilosort` line;
clustering "based on the original MATLAB version of Kilosort
2.5, written by Marius Pachitariu"). The modifications that
matter:

    Destriping. Neuropixels traces have common-mode "stripes"
    from the ADC multiplexing. IBL removes them before
    detection. If you skip this, Kilosort will invent units
    that are electrical clocks.

    Bad-channel detection. Dead, noisy, and out-of-brain
    channels are flagged and interpolated or excluded so they
    do not seed templates.

    Dense geometry passed in explicitly. The sorter is told
    the 16/20 µm checkerboard and the sample_shift, so a
    template can be a 2-D spatial footprint rather than 384
    unrelated traces.

    Automatic quality metrics after clustering, not Phy
    clicking. The BWM paper's 75,708 "good" neurons are not
    a human-curated subset. They are Kilosort clusters that
    passed the three numerical tests in the next subsection.
    There was no "a postdoc looked at this session in Phy
    and deleted the trash." That is a feature (reproducible,
    scalable across 699 insertions) and a limitation (a
    human would still split some merges and delete some
    noise the metrics missed).

    A single re-sort of the entire map. Acquisition spanned
    years. Early sessions were originally sorted with older
    IBL/Kilosort versions. For the public BWM freeze they
    re-ran the current pipeline on everything so that a unit
    in 2019 and a unit in 2022 mean the same algorithmic
    object. That re-run is what produced 621,733 Kilosort
    units, of which 75,708 passed QC.

The revision we loaded is SpikeSortingLoader revision
2024-05-06. That is a date stamp on the IBL Alyx/ONE object,
not a Kilosort version number. It is the sorted-spike release
that sits under clusters.pqt for the 2024_Q2_IBL_et_al_BWM
tag. If a later IBL release re-sorts again, cluster integers
will not match ours. We froze this revision by reading the
public aggregates rather than re-sorting.

How isolation actually happens, in English, because "Kilosort
clustered it" is not an explanation:

    A real spike is a stereotyped millivolt-scale (at the
    cell) / microvolt-scale (at the electrode) waveform that
    appears on a compact neighborhood of sites, with amplitude
    falling off with distance, and that cannot repeat inside
    a refractory period. Kilosort's templates are those
    stereotyped spatiotemporal movies. Two cells are isolated
    from each other when their movies are different enough —
    different shape, or different location on the probe, or
    both — that assigning a spike to the wrong template would
    make a worse reconstruction of the voltage. Cells that
    sit on top of each other with similar waveforms are the
    ones that get merged (contamination). Cells whose spikes
    sometimes look small (the cell is far, or the trough is
    near the detection threshold) are the ones that get
    truncated (false negatives). The three IBL tests below
    are numerical attempts to catch those two failure modes
    plus the "this template is just noise" mode, without a
    human.

    Isolation is therefore only as good as (a) the geometry
    being dense enough for a footprint, (b) drift correction
    keeping the footprint still, (c) the cell being close
    enough to a site for a large amplitude, and (d) no other
    cell looking the same. Tetrodes did (a) with 4 wires in
    a ~20 µm bundle and could isolate a handful of cells in
    a local cluster. Neuropixels does (a) along 3.84 mm and
    isolates hundreds, with the same logic stretched in
    depth. It does not give you a census of every cell in
    motor cortex. It gives you the ones that sat near the
    shank, were large enough, and were distinctive enough.

Known failure modes the white paper itself flags, which a
model should not pretend away:

    Overmerges (two cells, one cluster) and oversplits (one
    cell, two clusters, often because drift correction was
    imperfect).
    Units that are real but unstable, so they fail QC even
    though a human would keep them for a short window.
    Units that pass QC and are still multi-unit, because the
    refractory test is a statistical bound, not a proof.
    The sorter never sees the LFP band, so a "unit" has no
    idea whether it is firing at a preferred beta phase.

After sorting, IBL stores, per cluster, the peak channel, the
template, amplitudes, spike times, and the QC metrics. We
keep spike times (binned) plus the peak-channel anatomy.
We do not keep the templates.


1.4  Isolation QC: the three tests behind label = 1

The Brain-Wide Map paper did not use the classical "isolation distance"
or L-ratio cuts from older tetrode papers. They used the IBL's three
neuron-level tests, stored as a single score called `label` on each
unit. `label` is the fraction of those three tests the unit passed:
1 means all three, 2/3 means two, 1/3 means one, 0 means none. The
paper kept only units with label = 1 (in code, `label >= 1`). Out of
621,733 Kilosort units in the release, 75,708 passed. Those are what
the paper calls "well-isolated neurons," and often just "neurons."
Everything else is still in the raw sorting; it is not in their
analyses, and it is not in ours.

We used exactly that set. If we had invented a stricter or looser cut,
later numbers would not be comparable to theirs, and we would not know
whether a difference came from biology or from a different definition
of "a neuron."

Here is what the three tests actually ask, in English, then in the
numbers the code uses.

    1. Is the spike big enough to be a cell, not a wiggle?

       A real action potential on a Neuropixels probe is tens to
       hundreds of microvolts. Tiny events are usually noise or a
       distant cell you cannot isolate. The test: the median spike
       amplitude of the unit must be greater than 50 microvolts.
       Fail this, and the cluster is treated as noise.

    2. Does this look like ONE cell, or a pile of cells?

       A neuron cannot spike twice in a fraction of a millisecond.
       After a spike there is a refractory period: a short dead time.
       If a "unit" has lots of spike pairs closer together than that,
       you are not looking at one cell. You are looking at at least
       two cells whose waveforms got merged, or at noise.

       The catch is that different cell types have different
       refractory periods, and a slow cell produces so few spikes
       that a couple of close pairs might be chance. So IBL does not
       pick one magic dead-time (the old "2 ms ISI violation" rule)
       and fail you for exceeding it. They slide over possible
       refractory periods from about 0.5 to 10 milliseconds, in
       0.25 ms steps. At each candidate dead-time they ask: given
       this cell's firing rate, is the number of too-close spike
       pairs low enough that contamination is under 10%, with at
       least 90% confidence (Poisson assumption)? If ANY of those
       dead-times looks clean enough, the unit passes. If none does,
       it fails. Very low-rate units tend to fail even with few
       violations, because there is not enough evidence they are
       clean. That is intentional: "we cannot tell" is a fail, not
       a pass.

       Plain meaning: we only keep clusters that behave as if a
       single cell is firing, not a crowd.

    3. Did we catch all of this cell's spikes, or only the loud ones?

       Spike-sorting has a detection threshold. If a cell's spike
       heights straddle that threshold, the histogram of amplitudes
       looks chopped off on the left: lots of spikes piled against
       the floor, and an invisible pile of smaller spikes that were
       never detected. Those missing spikes will be counted as
       silence, which is a lie about the firing rate.

       The test looks at that amplitude histogram without assuming
       it is a bell curve. It compares the short left tail to the
       upper part of the distribution. The unit fails if the
       leftmost occupied bin is BOTH (a) more than 5 standard
       deviations above the upper-tail mean AND (b) more than 10%
       as tall as the peak bin. In code this is the "noise cutoff"
       metric; passing means `noise_cutoff < 5`. It is a
       dimensionless shape score, not a voltage.

       Plain meaning: we only keep clusters whose spike heights look
       fully above the detection floor, so "no spike in this 20 ms"
       can be trusted as the cell not firing.

A unit must pass all three to get label = 1. Passing amplitude and
refractory but failing the truncated-histogram test still yields
2/3, and the paper throws it out. They do not mix "pretty good"
units with "good" units.

Two caveats, so the methods text and the code do not fight:

    The Nature paper's Methods sentence summarizes the three tests
    as "amplitude > 50 μV; noise cut-off < 20 μV; and refractory
    period violation." The 50 μV amplitude cut is real. "Noise
    cut-off < 20 μV" is not what the released IBL code computes;
    the operational gate is the histogram metric with threshold 5,
    plus the sliding refractory-period test, plus 50 μV. When we
    say we used the paper's units, we mean we used `label == 1` as
    computed by that code and frozen in clusters.pqt, not a
    reimplementation of the slightly garbled Methods sentence.

    Other metrics exist (presence ratio, drift, missed-spike
    estimates, classical ISI contamination). IBL computes some of
    them. They are not part of `label`. Isolation distance and
    L-ratio are not the gate. If a later analysis wants those,
    they would be extra, and they would no longer be the BWM unit
    set.

The paper then applies two further filters that are NOT isolation
QC: drop units in `void` or `root` (not grey matter in the Allen
atlas), and for their canonical regional analyses keep a region
only if it has at least 5 well-isolated neurons in a session and
that region appears in at least 2 sessions. Our DatasetFiltered /
ModelData analog of the last idea is the "at least 5 MOp/MOs units
in the session" cut, applied at the motor-cortex admission ticket,
not as a redefinition of what a neuron is.


1.5  How a contact on the probe becomes a brain area: atlases,
     histology, electrophysiological alignment, and why a mouse
     map is not a monkey map

A channel index (0–383) is a location on silicon. A name like MOp5
is a location in a standardised mouse brain. Getting from one to
the other is a pipeline with millimetre-scale targeting error,
tens-of-micrometres alignment error, and a species-specific atlas
at the end. If you skip this, "motor cortex coverage" is a
stereotaxic hope. If you trust it blindly, you will treat a 70 µm
error as a layer boundary.

The atlas this paper uses is the Allen Mouse Common Coordinate
Framework, CCF v3 (Lein et al. 2007; Oh et al. 2014; Wang et al.
2020), paired with the Allen Reference Atlas ontology. The CCF is
a 3-D voxel grid of a standardised adult mouse brain, typically
sampled at 10 or 25 micrometres. Every voxel has:

    a millimetre coordinate (in IBL's convention: x = medio-
    lateral with negative = left, y = anterior-posterior,
    z = dorsal-ventral)
    an integer region id
    a human-readable acronym (MOp, MOs, CP, VAL, ...) and,
    in cortex, a layer suffix (MOp5, MOs6a, ...)

"Standardised" is doing a lot of work. Real mice are not the
average mouse. Brains differ in size, in the angle of the
cortical sheet, in how much a probe dimpled the surface, and in
how much histology itself warped the tissue. The CCF exists
because you cannot average "bregma −1.0, 0.5, 1.2 mm" across
animals and call that a region. You register each brain to the
template, then read the template's labels.

IBL's localisation workflow (Liu, Newsome, et al., eNeuro 2021,
"Accurate Localization of Linear Probe Electrode Arrays across
Multiple Brains"; Wool and IBL 2020; the IBL histology white
papers) has two stages, and confusing them is how people get
the error bars wrong.

    Stage 1 — find the track in the brain.

        The probe is painted with a fluorescent dye (CM-DiI)
        before insertion. After the recording, the animal is
        perfused, the brain is cut and imaged. IBL's in-house
        method is serial block-face two-photon microscopy
        (SBF2P): photograph the block face, cut a slice,
        photograph, repeat, so you get a 3-D volume in which
        the DiI track is a fluorescent streak. That volume is
        registered to the Allen average template. A human
        then traces the streak as a 3-D polyline of "picks"
        (xyz_picks). This is the insertion track in CCF
        coordinates. It says: the shank went along this
        curve. It does not yet say which electrode sat at
        which millimetre of that curve, because the brain
        was sliced after the probe came out, and because
        histology warps.

    Stage 2 — slide the 384 channels along that track until
    the electrophysiology matches the anatomy.

        Along a Neuropixels column you can see landmarks in
        the voltage without any histology: the brain surface
        (activity starts); white matter (few spikes, different
        LFP); ventricles (dead quiet); hippocampal CA1 (big
        spikes, LFP phase reversal); the hippocampus-thalamus
        border; cerebellar white matter; and so on. IBL's
        alignment GUI plots those electrophysiological
        features as a function of depth on the probe, plots
        the atlas regions along the reconstructed track, and
        lets a curator place corresponding landmarks. The
        software then interpolates, possibly stretching, the
        mapping from "axial_um on the shank" to "xyz in CCF."
        That mapping is applied to every channel, and the
        CCF voxel at that xyz becomes brain_area.

        This second step is why two insertions with the same
        stereotaxic aim do not have the same channel-to-area
        table, and why a unit's allen_z is not "the manipulator
        reading." Ground-truth checks with optogenetic and
        fluorescent tagging of known pathways put the
        electrode-to-atlas error at better than about 0.1 mm
        for this class of workflow (Liu et al. 2021). 100
        micrometres is several cortical layers. Layer labels
        (MOp5 vs MOp6a) are therefore informed guesses with
        real error, not facts with the same status as "this
        spike happened." Region labels at the scale of MOp
        versus MOs versus striatum are much safer: those
        structures are hundreds of micrometres to millimetres
        thick along a typical track.

IBL stores whether this alignment is `alignment_resolved`
(a curator finished the GUI, and in the BWM protocol a second
lab often looks at it). Units in `void` or `root` failed this
pipeline: they sit outside annotated grey matter, either
because the channel is out of the brain or because the
alignment put them in a hole. The paper drops them. So do we.

What we store per unit from this pipeline:

    brain_area     Allen acronym plus layer when the voxel
                   has one (MOp5, MOs2/3, MOs6a, ...). Our
                   motor ticket is "starts with MOp or MOs,"
                   which keeps all layers and drops FRP
                   (frontal pole), even though FRP sits next
                   door.
    allen_x, y, z  CCF millimetres. x < 0 is left hemisphere.
                   This is how LeftIpsi / RightContra know
                   laterality. It is also how you would plot
                   a unit inside MOp rather than just count
                   it as "one motor cell."
    axial_um       shank coordinate, used to order units
                   along the probe. Combined with brain_area
                   it is how you see the shank stacking
                   layers.

The mapping is per channel, inherited by the unit via the
unit's peak channel. A neuron whose extracellular field
straddles a region border is assigned to wherever its
largest-amplitude site sat. That is a convention.

Why this does not copy to other organisms, and what would
have to change.

    The CCF is a mouse atlas. Its named regions, its layer
    scheme, its voxel size, and its coordinate origin are
    defined for Mus musculus. A rat is mapped in Waxholm
    Space, a marmoset in its own MRI/atlas, a macaque in
    CHARM/SARM and related parcellations (Reveley, Saleem,
    Jung, and colleagues), a human in MNI space with
    atlases such as AAL, Harvard-Oxford, Glasser MMP, and
    so on. Those are different meshes of different brains.
    You cannot take a mouse allen_xyz, scale it by the
    ratio of brain weights, and land in human M1.

    Homology is by region name and by circuit, not by
    millimetre. Mouse MOp is the closest named analogue of
    primate primary motor cortex (M1 / F1 / area 4), mouse
    MOs of parts of premotor / secondary motor cortex. The
    analogue is imperfect: primate M1 has a giant Betz-cell
    layer 5 and a thumb-sized somatotopic map; mouse MOp is
    small, the map is cruder, and corticospinal density is
    different. If this project were in a macaque, "motor
    cortex" would be a different set of atlas labels, a
    different probe (the same 3.84 mm Neuropixels column
    would sit entirely inside a sulcal bank instead of
    traversing cortex plus striatum), and a different
    histology pipeline (often MRI + dye + registration to
    the macaque template, not Allen serial two-photon).

    Probe geometry is species-agnostic; coverage is not.
    A 10 mm NP 1.0 shank can span most of a mouse
    hemisphere's depth. In a human it is a local column.
    IBL's "brain-wide" claim is a mouse claim: the grid of
    insertions tiles the mouse CCF. A "brain-wide map" in
    another species is a different experimental design,
    even with the same silicon.

    Head-fixed Neuropixels in mice is IBL's recording
    regime. Freely moving Neuropixels, Utah arrays in
    primate M1, Neuropixels in rat medial agranular
    cortex, sEEG in human — all produce "spikes during
    movement" and none of them share an atlas. Combining
    them in one model without an explicit homology rule
    (this named area in atlas A corresponds to that named
    area in atlas B) is mixing coordinate systems.

    Even inside the mouse, strain, age, and sex shift the
    brain relative to the CCF. IBL uses a standardised
    wild-type line and still relies on per-animal
    registration rather than a look-up from bregma.

    Other mouse atlases exist (Paxinos and Franklin's 2-D
    plates, the Kim / Franklin 3-D variants). IBL committed
    to Allen CCF v3 so that a region name in a Mainz session
    and a region name in a Berkeley session are the same
    string. We inherited that commitment. If we had labelled
    motor cortex from Paxinos plates by eye, our MOp counts
    would not match the paper's, and laterality via allen_x
    would not exist.

Modelling consequences of the atlas step, stated as things a
model could use and currently mostly does not:

    brain_area as a categorical feature (MOp5 vs MOs6a vs
    everything else) is the coarsest, safest anatomical
    prior. Layer is less safe than region, as noted.

    allen_xyz as a continuous feature lets a model share
    strength across nearby tissue and across animals
    ("a unit at this CCF location tends to..."), which is
    the whole point of a common framework. It also lets
    you plot residuals in the brain rather than as a bar
    of session IDs.

    axial_um plus probe id is the right coordinate if the
    scientific claim is about the recording column (CSD,
    laminar current, neighboring-unit smoothness), not
    about named areas.

    Alignment error is a source of label noise. A handful
    of our "MOp" units may be MOs or somatosensory cortex
    (SSp, which borders MOp). That is one reason we did
    not subset spike_counts to motor units only for the
    saved tensors: a decoder can still use neighbors, and
    a later analysis can tighten the mask.

    Laterality is atlas-derived. allen_x < 0 is left
    because that is the CCF convention IBL adopted, which
    matches the histological left of the animal. It is
    not "the probe was in the left craniotomy" — a right-
    hemisphere insertion has allen_x > 0 even if the
    stereotaxic notes were sloppy. KS023 2019-12-10 is
    the existence proof: motor units with allen_x > 0 on
    a mouse that also has left-hemisphere probes.

The short version: a unit is a Kilosort template on a 384-
channel checkerboard, QC'd by three tests, sitting at a CCF
voxel that a DiI track plus an ephys-to-atlas alignment
assigned to it, in a mouse-specific atlas that does not
transfer by scaling to any other species. spike_counts is
the 20 ms compression of that object. Everything else the
electrode measured is still on disk at IBL and is not in
these folders.


A "firing rate" is how often a unit spikes, usually expressed in Hertz
(spikes per second). Motor-cortex cells in this dataset have a median
rate of a few Hertz, which is typical: they are not firing constantly,
and a 20 millisecond bin will often contain zero or one spike from a
given cell. That is why we count spikes in bins rather than treating the
raw point process as a dense video. It is also why you need a population
of cells, not one cell, to reconstruct a continuous movement: a single
neuron is a flickering, noisy vote.

"Encoding" is the claim that some property of the world (or of the body)
can be read out from those spike times. "The neuron encodes wheel speed"
is everyday language for "if I know the spikes, I can predict wheel
speed better than chance, and if I know wheel speed, I can predict the
spikes better than chance." Encoding is a statistical relationship. It
does not mean the neuron is causally driving the wheel, and it does not
mean the wheel is what the neuron "cares about." A neuron in your motor
cortex that fires when you turn a doorknob will statistically encode the
angular velocity of the knob. The cell is not a knob detector. It is
closer to a muscle-and-limb detector, and the knob is what the limb
happened to be holding.

That distinction — statistical readout versus the thing the circuit is
actually computing — is the entire reason this dataset exists.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
2. Motor cortex, in plain language
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The cerebral cortex is the wrinkled outer sheet of the mammalian brain.
Different patches of it are specialized. Visual cortex is specialized
for the eyes. Motor cortex is specialized for movement.

In the mouse, the two motor areas we care about are named in the Allen
Brain Atlas (CCF v3; how a Neuropixels channel gets one of these names
is section 1.5 — it is histology plus electrophysiological alignment,
not the manipulator reading):

    MOp    primary motor cortex
    MOs    secondary motor cortex

"Primary" does not mean "the only place movement is generated." It means
this patch has a relatively direct path to the spinal cord, where motor
neurons live that actually contract muscles. Many of those output cells
sit in deep layers, which is why you will see area names like MOp5 and
MOs6a in our unit table: 5 and 6a are cortical layers, stacked like
floors of a building. Layer 5 in motor cortex is famous for corticospinal
cells. Layer 2/3 is more corticocortical. We kept all of those layers
because a probe records whatever it passes through, and because
"movement" is not only the last-stage command.

A crude but useful fact about the wiring: each hemisphere of motor cortex
preferentially controls the opposite side of the body. Left MOp is more
about the right limbs; right MOp is more about the left limbs. This is
called contralateral control. It is not absolute — there is ipsilateral
and bilateral influence, especially for posture and for skilled
manipulation — but it is the textbook first approximation.

That fact will come back to haunt ModelDataLeft. Most of the motor units
in this dataset sit in the LEFT hemisphere. The trials we kept are LEFT
paw trials. Classically, that is the ipsilateral pairing, not the
contralateral one. We did not hide that. Section 13 discusses it.

Motor cortex is not a keyboard where each key is a muscle. It is closer
to a dynamical system that generates patterns. Those patterns get
translated, several synapses downstream, into muscle activations. The
patterns can represent:

    intended direction of a reach
    the speed or vigor of a movement
    the current posture being held
    a rhythmic or repeated action
    the switch from "hold still" to "go"
    preparatory activity that happens BEFORE any muscle twitches
    feedback about how the limb is actually moving
    the presence of an object the limb is contacting

A single scalar like "wheel angular velocity" collapses all of that into
one number. Sometimes that number is a decent summary. Often it is not.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
3. What the mouse is asked to do (the IBL task)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The International Brain Laboratory (IBL) is a consortium of labs that
agreed to run the SAME mouse task and share the data. The task is a
visual decision.

A mouse sits with its head fixed, so the skull cannot move. That is
necessary for a Neuropixels probe, which cannot travel with a freely
walking animal at micron precision. The mouse's front paws can still
move. In front of the paws is a small steering wheel, like a tiny
steering wheel from a car, mounted so that rotating it left or right is
the animal's way of answering.

On each trial:

    1. A visual stimulus appears on the left or the right of a screen.
       The stimulus is a Gabor patch (a fuzzy striped blob). How visible
       it is is called contrast, which we stored as "brightness":
       0%, 6.25%, 12.5%, 25%, or 100%. Zero percent is a catch: there is
       no visible blob, but a trial still happens.

    2. The mouse must report the side by turning the wheel that way.

    3. If it is correct, it gets a drop of sugar water (reward).
       If it is wrong, it does not.

    4. The world is not fair on every trial. Sometimes left is more
       likely (80%), sometimes right is more likely (80%), sometimes
       fifty-fifty. That block structure is "probabilityLeft," which we
       stored as block_prior. It exists so that the mouse (and anyone
       decoding its brain) has a prior, not just a stimulus.

The wheel turn is the REPORT of the decision. It is not the decision
itself. The decision is a computation that started when the stimulus
appeared, ran through visual cortex and decision-related areas, and
ended, if the animal is doing the task, in a movement. The BWM paper is
largely about that whole-brain computation: where can you read out the
choice, how early, how does contrast affect it. Movement, for that
paper, is mostly a timestamp (when did the wheel first move) and a
one-dimensional trace (how fast is the wheel spinning).

Watch a video of an actual trial, though, and you do not see a wheel
spinning itself. You see a paw. Sometimes one paw. Sometimes both.
Sometimes the paw is planted on the wheel and dragging it. Sometimes the
paw lifts off, repositions, and plants again. Sometimes the other paw
is held against the body and twitching without ever touching the wheel.
Sometimes the animal is still for a few hundred milliseconds after the
stimulus, then explodes into a turn. Sometimes it fidgets first.

That is the behavior we are trying to put back into the dataset.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
4. What the Brain-Wide Map paper actually did
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The paper (International Brain Laboratory et al.) recorded Neuropixels
probes across many brain regions, in many labs, in the task above, and
asked which regions carry which task variables.

Two analyses matter for us.

Decoding. Take spike counts in a window of time, train a statistical
model, and ask how well you can predict a variable the experimenter
already knows: the stimulus side, the contrast, the mouse's choice, or
the wheel velocity. If a region decodes choice well, the paper treats
that as evidence that the region "has" choice information.

Encoding. The reverse direction: predict a neuron's firing from the
task variables, often with a GLM (generalized linear model). This asks
what the cell's rate depends on.

For movement, their workhorse target was the wheel. Specifically, for
the decoding analysis that our 20 ms grid is in conversation with:

    They took a FIXED time window around the first wheel movement:
        firstMovement minus 0.2 seconds
        to firstMovement plus 1.0 seconds
    That is 1.2 seconds exactly.
    They chopped it into 20 millisecond bins.
    1.2 / 0.02 = 60 bins, every trial, every mouse.
    Wheel velocity was linearly interpolated onto that grid.
    Spikes were counted inside each bin.
    A decoder then tried to reconstruct the wheel trace from the spikes.

That design is elegant for a multi-lab map:

    The wheel is logged in every session at 1000 Hz. Video is not.
    The wheel is one number over time, not a 3D skeleton.
    The first-movement-aligned window puts every trial into the same
        "peri-movement" frame, which is how you compare a visual-cortex
        insertion in Lab A to a motor-cortex insertion in Lab B.
    Sixty bins is a rectangular tensor. Machine-learning tools like
        rectangular tensors.
    The wheel IS the animal's answer. If you want "where is the
        decision's output," wheel velocity is that output's physical
        shadow.

None of that is foolish. It is what you do if your scientific object is
the decision, and movement is how the decision leaves the animal.

It is the wrong object if your scientific object is the movement itself.

They also restricted trials with a flag called bwm_include and with a
reaction-time window of 0.08 to 2.0 seconds (time from stimulus onset to
first wheel movement). They restricted neurons to well-isolated units
(label = 1): the cluster had to pass all three IBL isolation tests
(spike big enough, refractory period like one cell, amplitude histogram
not chopped off at the floor). Section 1 spells those tests out; section
10.8 has the numbers. We inherited all three of those filters, on
purpose, so that when we say "these are BWM trials and BWM units," we
mean it.

What they did not do:

    They did not treat paw position as a decoding target.
    They did not label whether the paw was on the wheel, off the wheel,
        or still.
    They did not ask which paw did the work.
    They did not let trial length vary with how long the animal actually
        took to get the reward. They cut a 1.2 s cookie from around
        firstMovement instead.
    They did not need video quality control, stereo views, or a theory
        of motor primitives, because the wheel does not care about any
        of that.

Our dataset starts where theirs stops.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
5. Why neural activity should not be thought of as encoding the wheel
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

This is the load-bearing section. Read it slowly.

5.1 The wheel is not a body part

Motor cortex's downstream targets are, eventually, muscles. Muscles
move bones. Bones, in this task, are the bones of the mouse's forelimb.
The wheel is an object those bones sometimes press on.

There is no "wheel muscle." There is no sensory nerve from the wheel
into motor cortex. There is, at most:

    proprioception from the limb (sensors in muscles and joints that
        say how the arm is configured)
    touch from the paw pads if they contact the wheel
    vision of the wheel, which is mostly a visual-cortex problem
    the mechanical consequence that when the paw drags, the wheel
        rotates, and that rotation is what the experimenter scores

If a motor-cortex population statistically predicts wheel speed, it is
almost certainly because wheel speed is a FUNCTION of limb state during
the moments when the paw is planted. The chain is:

    motor cortex
        -> spinal cord
        -> muscles
        -> limb kinematics
        -> frictional contact
        -> wheel angular velocity

Decoding the last item and calling it "what motor cortex encodes" is
like decoding the speed of a shopping cart from the driver's motor
cortex and concluding that motor cortex represents shopping carts. You
will get a good decoder on days when the driver is pushing the cart.
You will get a terrible decoder — or a systematically lying one — on
days when the driver lets go, lifts the cart, or walks beside it.

5.2 The mapping from limb to wheel is many-to-one, and sometimes
    one-to-nothing

Many different limb trajectories can produce the same wheel velocity:
different contact points, different force profiles, one paw or two,
a plant-and-drag versus a series of short shoves. The wheel is a one-
dimensional projection of a higher-dimensional action.

Worse: many limb trajectories produce ZERO wheel velocity. Lifting the
paw, hovering, switching paws, a postural adjustment, a missed swipe —
all of these are movements. The wheel says "nothing is happening." A
decoder trained to output wheel speed is being trained to treat those
movements as the same as sitting still.

That is not a small edge case. In the left-paw subset we kept, the first
half of a typical trial is still dominated by "still," and coupled
episodes are a minority of time. Decoupled movement exists. If you only
look at the wheel, decoupled movement is invisible, and still is
indistinguishable from "I am moving my arm in the air."

5.3 What would have to be true for the wheel to be the right target

The wheel would be a sufficient movement variable if, and only if, all
of the following were approximately true:

    (a) The paw is always in contact with the wheel during the window
        you decode.
    (b) That contact is always mechanically effective (no slip, no
        pushing in a non-rotating direction).
    (c) The animal always uses the same effector (same paw, same
        posture).
    (d) You do not care about movement that is not the report.
    (e) You are willing to treat motor cortex as an "output cable for
        the decision" rather than as a controller of a limb.

For the BWM paper's question — can we read the choice out of region X —
(d) and (e) are close to the point, and (a)–(c) are nuisances they can
average over with 30,000 trials. For a motor question, (a)–(c) ARE the
phenomenon, and (d)–(e) are the mistakes.

5.4 Why the original authors still used it (and why that was not a
    blunder)

We are not claiming the BWM authors were confused about anatomy. They
knew the wheel is a manipulandum. They used it because:

    It is the task-relevant output. In decision neuroscience, the
        "movement" is often defined as whatever implements the choice.
        A saccade, a button press, a joystick flick, a wheel turn: the
        tradition is to take the report and decode it.
    It is standardized. Twelve labs, one wheel protocol. Video
        pipelines differ; wheels do not.
    It is low-dimensional. You can plot a single trace. You can put it
        in a GLM. You can compare labs.
    It is already QC'd. Pose tracking fails. Wheels rarely do.

Those are engineering and scientific-community reasons. They are good
reasons to use the wheel as a PROXY. They are not reasons to believe
motor cortex represents wheel radians per second as such.

Our project is what happens when you stop treating the proxy as the
thing.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
6. Why the paw is the right object
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

6.1 The paw is what the muscles move

The mouse's report is implemented by a forelimb action. The most distal
segment we can see well on the IBL side cameras is the paw (the tracker
labels a keypoint called paw_r on each camera; "r" here means "the paw
closer to this camera," not "right paw" — a confusing bit of IBL
vocabulary that section 10.2 spells out). If motor cortex is generating
a command, the first place that command should show up in the external
world is in the limb's kinematics: position, velocity, and whether the
paw is planted or lifted.

Predicting paw speed from spikes is therefore a more honest motor
decoding problem than predicting wheel speed. You are asking the model
to reconstruct something the descending pathway actually influences,
rather than something that pathway influences only when a contact
constraint happens to hold.

6.2 Paw kinematics dissociate from the wheel exactly when the science
    is interesting

Three situations:

    STILL. Paw not really moving, wheel not really moving. A wheel
        decoder and a paw decoder agree: nothing is happening. Even
        here the neural story may not be "zero." Holding a posture is
        an active computation (more in section 7).

    COUPLED. Paw moving, in contact, wheel moving. This is the regime
        in which the BWM wheel target is a decent proxy. A paw-speed
        decoder and a wheel-speed decoder should both work, and they
        should be correlated, because the limb is doing the task.

    DECOUPLED. Paw moving, wheel not (or not because of this paw). A
        wheel decoder is being asked to output near-zero while motor
        cortex may be highly active. A paw decoder is being asked to
        output the actual limb speed. This is the regime that tells
        you whether your "motor" decoding was ever about the limb, or
        only about the report.

If those three regimes are mixed together in training, a model can take
a rotten shortcut: ignore the limb, predict the wheel, look good on
average because coupled periods dominate the error when the wheel is
moving, and silently fail on decoupled movement. You would never notice
if you only reported wheel-decoding R^2, which is what a BWM-style
analysis reports.

6.3 The paw has more than one number

The wheel is a signed scalar (left vs right rotation, and how fast).
The paw, even in our reduced representation, has:

    x, y in the near-camera image plane, in left-camera-equivalent
        pixels (right-camera 640×512 traces are multiplied by 2)
    z as a stereo disparity (a mediolateral proxy, not millimetres),
        rebuilt after that scale so z = x_near − x_far in the same
        pixel units
    vx, vy, vz
    speed = hypot(vx, vy)     2D image-plane magnitude; z is not in it
    speedNormalized           that speed divided by the session's
                              typical coupled speed
    a discrete regime label

That extra dimensionality is not a nuisance. Direction, height above
the wheel, and whether you are planted are different motor problems.
Primary motor cortex in other species is famous for directional tuning
during reaches, for posture, and for contact. Collapsing to wheel
radians throws those axes away.

We still often talk about predicting "paw speed" as a first target
because speed is the simplest scalar that is about the limb and not
about the object. That is a starting point, not a claim that motor
cortex is a speedometer.

7. Ballistic tasks versus this task: why the usual playbook fails
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

A great deal of what "everyone knows" about motor cortex comes from
tasks that are nothing like a mouse on an IBL wheel.

7.1 What a ballistic movement task is

Think of a monkey reaching from a center hold to one of eight targets
(the "center-out" task). Or a rodent making a single, stereotyped grab
for a pellet. Or a human pointing at a dot. The properties those tasks
are designed to have:

    A discrete GO. The animal is still, then it moves, then it is
        still again.
    A stereotyped trajectory. Trial 17 to the upper-right target looks
        like trial 43 to the upper-right target. You can average them.
    A short, once-and-done movement. The interesting neural event is a
        burst around onset, not a long conversation with an object.
    An effector that is specified. Right arm. Always.
    An experimenter-defined peri-movement window that actually contains
        the movement and not much else.

Because of that design, a set of analysis habits grew up and became
"how you do motor neuroscience":

    Align to movement onset.
    Time-warp trials so that onset and target-hit fall on the same
        two ticks, even if some reaches were faster.
    Average the warped traces ("peri-event time histogram").
    Fit directional tuning: this cell likes 45 degrees.
    Treat the movement as one thing with a direction and a reaction
        time.

Those habits are not wrong for ballistic tasks. They are matched to the
behavior. Time-warping is almost defensible when every trial is the
same action at a different speed, the way two performances of the same
piece of music can be stretched onto one score.

7.2 What the IBL mouse is actually doing

Head-fixed, paws on or near a wheel, visual decision, then a turn that
may be:

    a single plant-and-drag
    several small corrections
    a lift, a reach, a plant, a drag
    a switch from one paw to the other
    a period of stillness, then a late explosion
    bimanual for some mice, unimanual for others
    interrupted by fidgets that have nothing to do with the report

The duration from stimulus to reward is not a constant. In our filtered
set the number of 20 ms bins per trial ranges from 8 (160 ms) to
pathological tens of thousands, with a median of 18 (360 ms) and a long
right tail. Even after we cap at 500 bins, ModelDataLeft still spans 8
to 494 bins. That is not "the same reach, sometimes faster." That is
different amounts of behavior.

There is no single "the movement" to warp onto. If you stretch a 280 ms
trial and a 2 s trial onto 60 bins, you are asserting that they are
the same action at different tempos. They are not. The long trial may
contain a hesitation, a lift, a second attempt, a postural hold. Those
are extra primitives, not a slow-motion replay of the short trial.

Reaction time itself (actionTime, stimulus to first wheel movement) is
already variable, which is why the BWM paper needed an 0.08–2.0 s
window. Everything AFTER first movement is even less stereotyped,
because that is where the interaction with the wheel lives.

7.3 Why this is a harder scientific problem, not just a messier dataset

In a ballistic reach, motor cortex can get away with a feedforward
burst: plan, go, brake. Feedback exists, but the movement is over
before much of it can change the plan.

On the wheel, the limb is in a closed loop with an object. Contact can
start and stop. Slip can happen. The animal can add a second paw. The
controller, if it is any good, is not replaying a tape. It is switching
between holding, engaging, and disengaging. Those switches are exactly
what still / coupled / decoupled are trying to name.

A decoder that assumes one regime — "the animal is always doing the
wheel-turn" — will misattribute neural activity from the other regimes.
A paper that only ever decodes the wheel will never find out.

7.4 Why we refused the two standard "make the tensor rectangular"
    tricks

Time-warp to a fixed number of bins. This is the ballistic habit. We
did not do it, because paw speed is a physical quantity in real time
(left-camera-equivalent pixels per second, or, after
speedNormalized, a dimensionless multiple of that session's typical
coupled drag). A 20 ms bin of spike counts is also a real-time quantity. Warping
would mean the same motor command is sometimes called "fast" and
sometimes "slow" depending on how long the trial was. It would also
smear still, coupled, and decoupled into each other whenever a long
trial has extra holds.

Pad every trial to the global maximum length. This keeps real time but
fills most of the tensor with fake time. The global maximum in the
unfiltered discretized set was 65,004 bins, from broken feedback
timestamps, not from a mouse thinking for twenty minutes. Even a
sane pad (say 500 bins) would mean most trials — median 18 bins — are
almost entirely padding. Models hate that unless you mask carefully,
and padding invents a "post-reward" or "pre-stim" epoch we did not
want to pretend we measured.

What we did instead: keep real 20 ms bins from stimulus onset to
reward, let n_bins vary, drop leftover time shorter than one bin, and
push the variable-length problem into the model. That is not because
variable length is convenient. It is because the behavior is variable
length, and hiding that would be lying about the motor problem.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
8. Still, coupled, and decoupled: intermediate primitives
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

This labeling is not in the BWM paper. It was built from watching
videos (the ZFM-01576 overlay code) and then applied to every trial.
It is the most interpretation-heavy object in the dataset, and it is
the one that most needs a scientific defense, not just a threshold
defense.

8.1 Why "the movement" is the wrong unit

Human motor neuroscience learned, slowly, that complex action is not
one trajectory. It is a sequence of simpler things: hold this posture,
launch this reach, grasp this object, release, hold again. Those
simpler things are called primitives (or synergies, or motor schemas,
depending on which decade and which lab). The claim is not that the
brain has three magic labels in a lookup table. The claim is that the
controller reuses a small number of control modes, and that mixing
those modes in your analysis is how you convince yourself motor cortex
is inscrutable.

On the IBL wheel, a trial is not "a left turn" the way a center-out
trial is "a 45-degree reach." A trial is a miniature sequence. The
sequence is composed of something like:

    hold the current pose (still)
    move the limb through free space, possibly toward the wheel
        (decoupled)
    engage the wheel and drag it (coupled)
    maybe disengage, maybe re-engage
    maybe use the other paw
    stop

"Left choice" is the task's name for the whole sequence. It is a
terrible name for what motor cortex has to generate at each moment.

8.2 STILL is not "the neuron is off"

Still means: the paw is not moving much, and we are not claiming a
drag. In the image, the paw is roughly where it was.

That can be:

    a true rest, waiting for the stimulus to be processed
    an active postural hold (isometric force against the wheel or
        against the body, with little visible motion)
    a pause between two drags
    the late part of a trial after the wheel has already been brought
        to criterion

Motor cortex is well known, in other species, to fire for posture.
A cell can have a preferred arm configuration and sit at a high rate
while nothing is moving. If you only decode velocity, you call that
cell "unrelated to movement" or you call still periods "baseline" and
subtract them away. Either move can be a mistake. Still is a regime
to predict, or at least to condition on, not a void.

In ModelDataLeft, still dominates the early trial. That is
neuroscientifically expected: decision computation is happening,
corticospinal output is not yet launching a drag, or the drag has not
started. A model that is given the whole trial and asked to predict
paw speed will spend most of its early bins predicting near-zero.
That is not a failure of motor encoding. That is the animal not having
moved yet. Conditioning on regime, or on time relative to first
movement, is how you stop a model from getting a cheap score for
saying "zero" for 200 ms.

8.3 COUPLED is interactive control, not a reach

Coupled means: the paw is moving, the wheel is moving, and the paw is
not lifted off the contact height. In English: the limb is engaged
with the object and the object is doing the thing the experimenter
cares about.

This is the closest analogue to "the BWM movement." It is also the
regime in which:

    tactile feedback from the paw pads should be live
    the mechanics of the wheel (inertia, friction, the rotary encoder)
        matter to the controller
    force, not just kinematics, is part of the problem — and we do
        not have force. We have kinematics plus the wheel's motion as
        a noisy consequence of force. That is a limitation. Coupled
        is where that limitation hurts most.
    a wheel decoder SHOULD work, and its success should not be taken
        as evidence that cortex represents the wheel

Coupled is closed-loop. Compare to a ballistic reach, which can be
mostly open-loop: muscle pattern launched, limb flies, small
corrections. Here the controller is in contact with something that
pushes back. That is a different spinal and cortical problem
(think "manipulate" versus "point").

If motor cortex has anything like a contact mode or a grasp mode —
and in primates, motor and premotor cortex absolutely do — coupled
is where it should light up as such, not merely as "high velocity."

8.4 DECOUPLED is movement that the task pretends does not exist

Decoupled means: the paw is doing something that is not turning the
wheel. Lifted off. Moving in the air. A mostly vertical swipe. Moving
while the wheel is idle.

This is free space movement. It is the closest analogue to a reach.
It is also, from the experimenter's point of view, often "not the
behavior." From the spinal cord's point of view it is absolutely the
behavior.

Why it must be separated:

    A wheel-only analysis codes it as zero output. If cortex is active,
        you conclude cortex is noisy, or "not motor," or encoding
        something cognitive. You have mislabeled a motor epoch as a
        cognitive residual.

    A paw-speed analysis without a regime label mixes it with coupled
        drags. Speeds may look similar; the mechanics are not. One is
        unloaded (the paw's mass). One is loaded (the wheel). Motor
        cortex and cerebellum care about load. Mixing them is how you
        get mushy tuning.

    Decoupled is often the REPOSITIONING primitive that sets up a
        coupled drag: lift, move, plant. If you only study coupled,
        you have thrown away the approach. If you only study the
        wheel, you have thrown away the approach AND miscounted it as
        rest.

    In some mice the "other" paw is decoupled almost always: it moves
        a little, held against the body, never plants. That movement
        is a confound for used_paw (which looks at speed) and a reason
        we built wheel_paws (which looks at coupling). Decoupled on
        the non-acting paw is not a motor report. It is a tell.

8.5 Why they should be encoded differently (the actual neural claim)

You cannot see the encoding until you train a model. The claim, stated
as a hypothesis this dataset is built to test, is:

    Still, coupled, and decoupled are not three speeds. They are three
    control problems, and motor cortex (and the rest of the recorded
    brain) should look different across them even at matched limb
    speed.

Concretely:

    Still versus moving is the classic peri-movement contrast: a
        condition-independent "go" or "hold" signal, plus rate changes
        in corticospinal cells. This exists in every ballistic dataset.
        We should see it here too, but "moving" must be split.

    Coupled versus decoupled, at the same paw speed, is the contact /
        load / object-interaction contrast. Predicted differences:
            tactile-related activity (more coupled)
            different muscle synergies (dragging in one axis versus
                lifting in another)
            possibly more feedback-driven activity when coupled,
                because the wheel is a mechanical loop
            possibly more feedforward reach-like activity when
                decoupled
        A wheel decoder cannot pose this contrast. A paw-speed decoder
        that ignores regime will average it away.

    Transitions (still to decoupled, decoupled to coupled, coupled to
        still) may be the most informative bins in the trial. Motor
        cortex is good at switching. If you bin at 20 ms you have a
        chance of seeing switches; if you average a 1.2 s window you
        do not.

    Other brain areas in the same session should split differently.
        Visual cortex should care about the stimulus, not the regime,
        except insofar as movement produces visual flow. Striatum
        might care about vigor and about whether the report is being
        executed (coupled) versus prepared. Thalamus will depend on
        which nucleus. This is why we kept non-motor units in the
        pickles even though admission to ModelData required motor
        cortex: the contrast across areas is part of the question.

8.6 These primitives compose; they are not the whole theory

Still / coupled / decoupled is a THREE-state cartoon of a richer
action. We do not have:

    force
    EMG (muscle electrical activity)
    a true 3D millimetre skeleton
    digits (the paw is one keypoint, not a hand)
    a "which joint" breakdown
    an experimenter-verified plant versus slip

So we should not pretend we have discovered the motor alphabet. We
have a coarse, video-based, mechanically motivated partition that
separates "not moving," "moving the object," and "moving but not the
object." That is already a different scientific object than "the
wheel trace," and it is matched to what the cameras can support.

The heuristic itself (thresholds, 80 ms, 100 px/s, 0.40 ratio) is
documented in Part II. Those numbers are judgement. The existence of
the three regimes is not a number. It is visible on the videos in
BehaviorRes/PawStrats. If you hate the thresholds, watch the videos
and propose better ones. Do not go back to pretending the wheel is
the limb.

9. What we kept from the paper, and why we kept it
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

A dataset that "improves on" a paper by silently changing the trial
set is not an improvement. It is a different experiment. We wanted
to be able to say: these are the BWM mice, the BWM trials, the BWM
units; we changed the MOVEMENT VARIABLE and the TIME WINDOW.

Kept, on purpose:

    The motor-cortex cohort of the 2023-12 freeze, not a private
        list of "sessions we like."
    bwm_include trials, so we are not quietly using trials the paper
        rejected for QC reasons.
    Reaction time 0.08–2.0 s, same as their decoding set. Below
        80 ms is too fast to be a decision-related movement in this
        task (it is often a fidget that happened to trip the wheel).
        Above 2 s is a mouse that did not engage.
    Units with clusters.pqt label >= 1 and spike-sorting revision
        2024-05-06. Same neurons they would have called neurons:
        median amplitude > 50 µV, sliding refractory-period
        contamination under 10% at 90% confidence, and an amplitude
        histogram that is not chopped off at the detection floor
        (noise_cutoff < 5). Label is the fraction of those three
        tests passed; we keep only a perfect score.
    20 ms bins. Not because 20 ms is sacred (cortical timescales
        range from a few milliseconds at the synapse to hundreds of
        milliseconds for decisions), but because it is their decoding
        currency, it is long enough that a 3 Hz cell sometimes has a
        spike in a bin, and it is short enough to see a drag start.
    Pose likelihood >= 0.95, the IBL default. Below that, the keypoint
        is a guess. Inventing a looser threshold would have invented
        fake paw motion, which is the one thing we cannot afford.
    Near-paw = paw_r on that camera, IBL's anatomical convention.
    Wheel traces as SessionLoader signed rad/s, their decoding
        representation, stored so we can still COMPARE paw decoding
        to wheel decoding on the same trials. We did not delete the
        proxy. We demoted it.

Changed, on purpose, and these are the scientific diffs:

    Target: paw kinematics and regime, not (only) wheel velocity.
    Window: stimulus onset to reward, not firstMovement−0.2 to +1.0.
        Why: we want the hold before the turn, the turn, and the
        finish, because those are different primitives. Their window
        is a peri-movement cookie that starts after still is mostly
        over and ends on a clock that does not care about reward.
    Length: variable n_bins, leftover dropped, no warp, no pad.
    No extrapolation of missing pose. Their decoder could extrapolate
        wheel because a 1.2 s window sometimes stuck out past the
        loaded wheel trace. Extrapolating a paw is fabricating a
        limb.
    Effector labels: used_paw, then wheel_paws, then a left-only
        subset. They had no effector.
    Speed units: right-camera ×2 into left-camera pixels, 2D
        hypot(vx, vy) not 3D, plus speedNormalized so sessions
        and cameras share a scale. They had no paw speed.
    Modeling copies that require pose, reward, stereo, enough motor
        units, visible contrast, and sane duration.

The next part is those copies, in the order they were made.

10.3 Trial inclusion: theirs, not ours

A trial is kept if ALL of:

    (a) bwm_include is True
    (b) actionTime = firstMovement − stimOn is in [0.08, 2.0] seconds
    (c) stimOn, feedback, firstMovement are finite
    (d) trialDuration = feedback − stimOn is > 0

Neuroscience of (b): a movement that starts 40 ms after a visual
stimulus is not a decision about that stimulus. Visual cortex has
barely seen it. 80 ms is a lower bound the paper chose to keep
"the first movement" in the space of reactions, not startles. 2 s is
an upper bound so that you are not decoding a mouse that disengaged.
We did not reopen that debate. If our paw decoder is compared to their
wheel decoder, the trials must match.

Error trials (4,312 of 30,264; feedbackType = −1) stay in Database.
An error is a different motor object: the animal may not complete a
turn, may turn the wrong way, may time out. The neural picture of
"failed report" is interesting and is not this modeling set. Dataset
Filtered drops them because the user asked for successful behavior and
because mixing successful drags with failed ones would again mix
regimes without naming them.

Clock: t = 0 at stimulus onset. That is a DECISION-ALIGNED clock, not
a MOVEMENT-aligned clock. The paper's wheel decoder used movement
alignment. We use stimulus alignment because we want the still period
before firstMovement to remain in the trial. That is the period during
which a ballistic analysis would already have thrown the data away,
and during which a decision analysis lives. Keeping both in one
window is how you later ask "when does the population switch from
decision-ish to motor-ish," which you cannot ask if you only ever
start at firstMovement − 0.2 s.

10.4 Stimulus, choice, reward, block

    stim_side     left or right (which side the Gabor was on)
    brightness    0, 0.0625, 0.125, 0.25, 1.0
    choice        left / right / nogo, from IBL integers 1 / −1 / 0
    block_prior   probabilityLeft: 0.2, 0.5, 0.8
    reward        1 or −1
    outcome       reward / error / no_feedback

Why these are not "just metadata." Contrast controls how hard the
decision is, and in this dataset it also controls how long the trial
is: low contrast, longer, more time in still. A motor decoder that
ignores contrast will confuse "hard trial, late movement" with "this
mouse has slow paws." Side of stimulus and side of choice are almost
always the same on rewarded trials (the animal got it right). Block
prior is a cognitive variable that should, in theory, affect when
movement starts more than how the paw is configured — another reason
to keep still in the window.

0% contrast is a different TASK (detect absence), not a dimmer Gabor.
It stays in Database and is removed at ModelData. See 10.F below.

10.5 The wheel, stored anyway

SessionLoader wheel at 1000 Hz, signed angular velocity in rad/s,
windowed to [stimOn, feedback], t shifted to 0 at stimOn.

We are arguing against the wheel as the TARGET. We still store it
because:

    it is how we DEFINE coupled (paw moving AND wheel moving)
    it is how we can run a BWM-style decoder as a baseline on the
        exact same bins
    it is the animal's report, which is still a scientifically real
        object, just not the motor object

If we had deleted the wheel we would have been unable to say "coupled."
The proxy is an input to the regime label, not the thing we want
cortex to mean.

10.6 Pose: two trackers, both cameras, one chosen paw at this stage

DeepLabCut (dlc) and Lightning Pose (lp) independently track body
parts on the left and right side cameras. IBL convention:

    On a given camera, paw_r is the NEAR paw (closer to that camera).
    left-camera  paw_r = anatomical LEFT paw
    right-camera paw_r = anatomical RIGHT paw
    The far view of the same paw is paw_l on the opposite camera.

Likelihood < 0.95 becomes NaN. We do not interpolate across long
holes. Inventing a paw position when the tracker is lost would invent
a movement, and we would then "decode" our interpolation.

The two side cameras are not a calibrated stereo pair, and they do
not even share a pixel grid:

    left camera    1280 × 1024    60 Hz     near paw = anatomical left
    right camera    640 ×  512   150 Hz     near paw = anatomical right

One right-camera pixel covers roughly twice the scene of one
left-camera pixel. Raw x and y are therefore not comparable across
paws until the right camera is multiplied by 2 (Step K). z is not
millimetres. It is z = x_near − x_far, a disparity proxy for
mediolateral position, rebuilt after that scale. There is no camera
calibration and no body-camera paw in this set. Claiming 3D
millimetres would have been theater.

Speed is 2D: hypot(vx, vy). It does not include vz. Folding disparity
into Euclidean speed would mix two rulers and let the noisier axis
dominate, especially on right-paw trials where z still contains the
finer left-camera x. z and vz remain stored as their own channels
if a later analysis wants mediolateral motion without pretending it
shares a metric with the image plane.

Two sessions had only one camera; they later leave the filtered set
because a modeling target that sometimes has z and sometimes does
not is two targets pretending to be one. Speed itself is 2D either
way; the stereo cut is so z is present as a feature, not so speed
can be 3D.

10.7 used_paw: a first, flawed effector label

We could not store both paws in the first pickles (size). For each
trial we picked the near paw with higher mean image-plane speed over
the trial, DLC first, Lightning Pose if needed, ties to left.

This is a MOVEMENT heuristic, not a dominance phenotype, and not
"who turned the wheel." A paw held against the body can wiggle fast
in pixels without doing the task. Videos showed that constantly.
used_paw is still on every trial because it is what the STORED
dlc_* / lp_* traces actually are: the kinematics of that chosen paw.

The later label wheel_paws is the scientific effector label. The
fact that they disagree on thousands of trials is not a bug. It is
the demonstration that "which paw moved" and "which paw did the
task" are different questions. Motor cortex should care about the
second more than the first if it is generating the report, and about
both if it is generating all movement.

10.8 Units and spikes

For each probe: SpikeSortingLoader revision 2024-05-06, intersect
with clusters.pqt label >= 1. Concatenate probes. unit_idx is 0 ..
n_units−1 for the session. Spikes on a trial are times relative to
stimOn plus an index into that table.

That `label >= 1` cut is the paper's isolation QC, not a private
SNR rule. IBL computes three binary tests and sets

    label = (number of tests passed) / 3

so the only values are 0, 1/3, 2/3, and 1. The paper's loader
(`load_good_units`, `min_qc=1`) keeps label == 1. We do the same.
The three tests, as implemented in
brainbox.metrics.single_units.compute_labels, are:

    (a) amp_median > 50 µV
        Median waveform height. Below this, the cluster is treated
        as noise. Plain English: the electrical event has to be
        large enough to be a nearby cell, not a ripple.

    (b) max_confidence >= 90 on the sliding refractory-period test
        Acceptable contamination 10%; bins 0.25 ms; candidate
        dead-times from ~0.5–10 ms. Pass if, for at least one
        candidate refractory period, we are at least 90% confident
        the unit is under 10% contaminated. Plain English: the
        spike train has to have a dead time, like one cell, not
        like two cells talking over each other. "Not enough spikes
        to tell" fails.

    (c) noise_cutoff < 5
        Amplitude-histogram shape test (quantile_length 0.25,
        100 bins). Fail if the low-amplitude bin is both > 5 SD
        above the upper-tail mean and > 10% of the peak-bin
        height. Plain English: the cluster cannot look chopped
        off at the detection threshold, or we would be mistaking
        missed spikes for silence.

The Nature Methods sentence says "amplitude > 50 μV; noise cut-off
< 20 μV; and refractory period violation." Use the code, not that
sentence, if they disagree: the 20 μV figure is not the operational
threshold. clusters.pqt already carries the frozen `label` from
this pipeline, which is why we read the label instead of
recomputing the three tests ourselves.

n_units is a session constant. A cell that does not fire on a trial
is a zero, not a missing column. That is the right neural fact:
silence is data. Dropping silent cells trial-by-trial would make
"population size" covary with "how much the animal moved," which
would confound any decoder. Isolation QC is applied once per unit
per session, not per trial: a neuron does not become "un-isolated"
because it was quiet on trial 17.

Coordinates: IBL CCF, x = ML (negative = left hemisphere), y = AP,
z = DV. Area strings like MOp5 are Allen acronyms plus layer. How
those strings and xyz values are produced from a DiI track, a serial
two-photon volume, and an ephys-to-atlas alignment — and why they
do not transfer by scaling to a rat, macaque, or human brain — is
section 1.5. The spike_counts tensor is sorted AP-band events only;
the LFP, MUA, waveforms, and CSD that the same 384 channels also
support are section 1.2, and they are not in these folders.

11. Step B — Velocity and the three regimes, at native camera time
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Script: /Users/Stefan/Desktop/enrich_and_discretize.py (first half)

Positions are not what muscles are commanded in. To a first
approximation, descending motor signals are closer to velocity and
force than to camera x. We compute, at the camera's own times:

    Gaussian-smooth each of x, y, z with sigma = 20 ms
    then np.gradient in time, only inside contiguous finite segments
        of at least 3 samples
    speed = hypot(vx, vy)

    (The first write of this step used 3D speed whenever vz was
    finite: sqrt(vx^2+vy^2+vz^2). That mixed image-plane pixels
    with disparity units. Step K replaced the stored speed arrays
    with the 2D hypot and did not delete the column.)

Why 20 ms smoothing: it matches the bin we will later use, and it
is a reasonable scale for suppressing camera jitter without turning
a 50 ms lift into mush. It is NOT the bin yet. Native time still
has ~60 Hz (left camera) or ~150 Hz (right camera) samples.

Then the regime machine, per sample, after interpolating wheel
velocity onto paw times:

    paw speed = hypot(vx, vy)     (image plane, for the state machine)
    v_still   = clip(max(0.12 * p95_speed, 140), 140, 320) px/s
    omega_move = clip(max(0.10 * p95_|wheel|, 0.30), 0.30, 1.00) rad/s

    contact baseline: exponential tracker on image y
        (y increases downward, so contact is LARGE y)
        follows downward quickly (40 ms) and upward slowly (280 ms)
        so a plant updates the "floor" fast and a lift is a drop
        below that floor
    lift_thr = max(14 px, 4.5 * early_y_std)

    STILL:      default, not moving much
    COUPLED:    moving AND wheel moving AND not lifted
    DECOUPLED:  lifted, OR moving while wheel idle, OR a mostly
                vertical swipe

Then a ~60 ms majority smoother and a 45 ms minimum dwell, so a
single noisy frame is not a regime change. Motor primitives, if they
are real, last longer than one video frame. If we had allowed
one-frame states we would have been labeling tracker noise.

Adaptive thresholds with floors: cameras and mice differ. A fixed
px/s cut is not a physical cut. The floors (140 px/s, 0.30 rad/s)
stop a dead-still trial from promoting jitter to "movement." This is
the same philosophical move as the paper's 80 ms minimum RT: do not
let noise become an event.

2D speed for the state machine, not 3D: coupling is a plant-and-drag
in the image, plus the wheel. z is noisier and sometimes missing.
Using z in the regime label would have made the label undefined on
exactly the sessions we were about to drop for missing z anyway.

These arrays (dlc_paw_state, lp_paw_state) are the native-time
primitives. Binning comes later. Computing them native-first is
deliberate: a regime that lasts 50 ms should be decided on 60 Hz
data, then voted into 20 ms bins, not invented inside a 20 ms bin
that has already averaged a plant and a lift together.

14. Step E — 20 ms discretization: their bin, not their window
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Outputs: DatabaseDiscretized and DatasetFilteredDiscretized.
ModelDataLeft descends from the latter.

14.1 Why 20 ms, again, now that you know about regimes

A spike is ~1 ms. A video frame is ~7–17 ms. A short coupled drag
might be 80–200 ms. A decision might be hundreds of milliseconds.
20 ms sits between frame rate and drag duration. It is coarse enough
that spike counts are not almost-all-zeros for a 3 Hz cell, and fine
enough that a still-to-coupled transition is not trapped inside a
200 ms sludge bin.

Could we have used 10 ms? Yes, at the cost of sparser counts. 50 ms?
Yes, at the cost of smearing primitives. We used 20 ms to stay in
the paper's currency so that "our paw decoder versus their wheel
decoder" is not also "our 50 ms versus their 20 ms."

14.2 Their window versus ours

They: firstMovement − 0.2 s to +1.0 s, always 60 bins.

That window is a ballistic habit. The −0.2 s captures a little
preparation. The +1.0 s captures a stereotyped-enough movement if
you believe movements last about a second. Many IBL trials do not.
Some are over in 200 ms. Some coupled drags start late and run long.
Their cookie both (i) includes time before stimOn on short-RT trials
and (ii) chops off the end of long trials, including extra primitives.

We: t = 0 at stimOn, through feedback (reward), n_bins =
floor(T / 0.02), leftover < 20 ms dropped.

    n_bins varies. That is the motor fact.
    Still at the beginning is in the data.
    We do not invent post-reward movement.
    We do not stretch dt away from 20 ms.

leftover_s is always in [0, 0.02). Values that look like 0.02 are
floating point.

14.3 Causality in the bin, and why motor people should care

    Spikes in bin i: t in [t_left, t_right)   (left-closed, right-open)
    Wheel and paw continuous traces: linearly interpolated at t_right

Every spike in the bin occurs strictly before the behavior sample
for that bin.

This is their decoding convention. It is also the ethically correct
one if you want to say "spikes predict movement" rather than "spikes
echo movement." Motor cortex can and does receive feedback, so some
activity is the consequence of the last 20 ms of motion, not the
cause of the next. We cannot solve that with binning. We can at least
not put future spikes into the features for this sample. A model that
still "predicts" well is then either truly predictive or using
feedback delayed by less than a bin — both of which are more
interesting than a model that cheats by seeing the paw's next frame
in the spike count.

No extrapolation. Pose holes stay NaN. Their wheel interpolator used
fill_value='extrapolate' because the 1.2 s window could stick out.
Extrapolating a paw speed is claiming a limb velocity you did not
see. For a motor target that is scientific misconduct in miniature.

14.4 States on the grid

Native still/coupled/decoupled are majority-voted inside each 20 ms
bin. Ties: sample closest to the right edge (consistent with "the
behavior sample lives at the right edge"). Empty: none / −1.

This is how a primitive that was computed at 60–150 Hz becomes a
20 ms label without being recomputed on already-averaged kinematics.
If a bin contains 10 ms of still and 10 ms of coupled, majority vote
is a hard choice; the min-dwell on the native labels already forbade
flicker, so mixed bins should be rare-ish and should cluster at
transitions — which are exactly the bins a model should not get a
confident regime for.

14.5 spike_counts

Shape (n_bins, n_units), uint16. Same n_units for every trial of a
session. Different n_bins across trials. Within a trial, every
timeseries has length n_bins. We checked all 23,783 filtered
discretized trials: zero within-row mismatches.

n_bins in that set: min 8, mode 14, median 18, p90 51, p99 ~300,
max 65,004 (broken timestamps). That max is why ModelData exists.

16.1 Why this label is the motor one

Somatotopy: motor cortex is laid out roughly by body part. Mixing
left-paw trials and right-paw trials in one decoder is mixing two
effectors, possibly two hemispheres' worth of control, and then
asking for one mapping from "the population" to "speed." In a
ballistic lab you would never pool left-arm and right-arm reaches
without saying so. The BWM wheel decoder does the equivalent by
accident, because the wheel does not say which paw turned it.

used_paw cannot fix this. Speed on the video does not equal task
work. The other paw is often moving, held to the body, never
planting. That is decoupled movement of a non-reporting limb. If you
call that trial "right" because the right paw wiggled more in pixels,
you then attach the RIGHT paw's stored kinematics to a trial whose
report was executed by the left paw. That is how you build a dataset
that cannot possibly yield a clean motor encoding.

So we reloaded BOTH near paws from OpenAlyx (left-camera paw_r and
right-camera paw_r), reused the still/coupled/decoupled machine, and
asked which paws spent real time in COUPLED.

16.2 The contribution rule

A paw contributes if:

    time in COUPLED >= 0.080 s     (~5 frames at 60 Hz)
    AND mean image-plane speed >= 100 px/s

If both contribute, label "both" only if
    min(coupled_s) / max(coupled_s) >= 0.40
Otherwise the weaker paw is a hitchhiker and the label is the
stronger one.

If neither contributes: fall back to used_paw (do not invent a third
philosophy).
If no pose: None. That is 1,682 Database trials, exactly the four
no-pose sessions. Zero unlabeled trials in ModelData.

80 ms: longer than the 45 ms min dwell, so a flicker of coupled is
not "this paw did the task." Short enough that a brief real plant
counts.

100 px/s mean: on ZFM-01576, the classic left-paw mouse, the other
paw's mean speed is ~8–21 px/s. Real reaches 160–340 px/s. 100 sits
between "body-held jitter" and "a drag." The instantaneous coupled
threshold is already ~140 px/s; the trial MEAN can be lower because
of still periods.

0.40 ratio: 80 ms of coupled on the passenger paw during a 400 ms
primary drag is not bimanual work. Diagnosed on three sessions
(ZFM-01576 left-dominant, ZM_2241 right-dominant, CSH_ZAD_026 mixed)
before running all 67.

Prefer Lightning Pose if it has a usable trace, else DLC, else
used_paw. LP's other-paw traces were cleaner (ZM_2241 other-paw
78 px/s DLC vs 35 px/s LP). In ModelDataLeft, all 7,173 trials have
wheel_paws_source == "lp".

We did not require DLC and LP to agree on "both." That would have
undercounted bimanual trials. We trusted LP. Videos in
BehaviorRes/PawStrats exist so a human can disagree.

19. Hemisphere, laterality, and whether this movement is ipsi or contra
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

This section is the detailed record of a question that sounds simple
and is not: does motor cortex control the wheel-turning paw from the
opposite side of the brain (contralateral), from the same side
(ipsilateral), or from both? And given how IBL actually planted the
probes, which of those pairings did we BUILD?

19.1 Plain-English anatomy, one more time

Each half of motor cortex preferentially talks to the opposite side
of the body. The main cable is the corticospinal tract. Most of its
axons cross the midline in the medulla (the pyramidal decussation)
and end in the spinal cord on the other side, where they influence
the motor neurons that contract limb muscles. Textbook:

    left MOp/MOs  ->  right paw
    right MOp/MOs ->  left paw

That is CONTRALATERAL control. Same-side (IPSILATERAL) axons exist.
They are a minority. They often go to more proximal/axial musculature
or are collaterals of cells that also project contralaterally. They
are not "the other textbook." They are the exception that skilled
and bimanual work keeps rediscovering.

Allen CCF in this dataset: allen_x < 0 is the left hemisphere,
allen_x > 0 is the right. No motor unit sits at x = 0.

19.2 What is actually in the recordings

Motor units in ModelData / ModelDataLeft / ModelDataRight (the unit
table is the same 2,464 cells in all three folders):

    Left hemisphere:  2,408 units  (97.7%)
        MOp  1,409   (every primary-motor cell we have)
        MOs    999
    Right hemisphere:    56 units  ( 2.3%)
        MOp      0
        MOs     56

    51 of 53 sessions: left motor cortex only
     1 session: right motor cortex only
        KS023 2019-12-10, 52 MOs, 0 MOp
     1 session: mixed
        CSHL058 2020-07-08, 66 left MOs + 4 right MOs

Layer mix, for the motor set as a whole: about 85% in layer 5 or 6a
(MOp5, MOs5, MOp6a, MOs6a), the output-ish layers. That does not
change by paw folder: we did not drop units when we copied.

This is not a biological finding about mice. It is IBL's insertion
grid. The BWM paper says so, in the sentence that designed the map:

    probes followed "a grid that covered the left hemisphere of the
    forebrain and midbrain, which typically represent stimuli or
    actions on the contralateral side, and the right hemisphere of
    the cerebellum and hindbrain, which typically represent the
    ipsilateral side."

They planted left cortex ON PURPOSE because they expected it to
represent the RIGHT side of the world and the RIGHT side of the
body. They planted right cerebellum on purpose because cerebellum
is ipsilateral. They did not plant right MOp as a matched control
for left-paw mice. We inherited that grid.

19.3 Units are a session property, so unique-unit "coverage by paw"
     cannot differ

Every ModelData session has at least one left-paw trial, one
right-paw trial, and (in this set) the unit list does not change
trial by trial. Therefore the UNIQUE motor units sitting in
ModelDataLeft are exactly the unique motor units sitting in
ModelDataRight: 2,408 left, 56 right. Asking "how many left-M1
cells are there on left-paw trials versus right-paw trials" as a
unique-unit count is a trick question. The answer is "the same
cells." Hemisphere was not assigned by paw.

What CAN differ is trial-weighted coverage: some sessions contribute
more left-paw trials, some more right-paw trials, and the two
sessions that even have right-hemisphere motor cells are not paw-
balanced. That is the next subsection.

19.5 What the Brain-Wide Map authors said about this

Three things, all in the 2025 Nature paper, and they pull in
different directions.

    (1) They DESIGNED for contralateral forebrain. The grid sentence
        quoted in 19.2 is an anatomical prior: left cortex should
        represent contralateral (right) actions. That is why
        ModelDataRight, not ModelDataLeft, is the folder that
        matches their design assumption.

    (2) They NOTICED that paw strategy varies, and that the movement
        is not ballistic. Quote, from the section on movement
        correlates:

            "We observed that different mice (and potentially the
            same mouse on different sessions) adopted different
            strategies for moving the wheel. For instance, some
            used both front paws, whereas others used only one paw.
            Turning the wheel is also a relatively complex
            operation, rather than being a simple, ballistic
            movement. Thus, one should not expect a simple
            relationship between these movements and activity in
            laterally specific motor areas. For simplicity, we
            restricted our analyses to the activity associated with
            wheel velocity (signed to distinguish left from right
            movements) and its absolute value."

        They saw the problem that ModelDataLeft / ModelDataRight
        exist to isolate. They then decoded the wheel anyway,
        explicitly because they did not want to take on lateralized
        paw control.

    (3) They never labeled which paw turned the wheel, never split
        analyses by effector, and never tested whether MOp/MOs
        activity was stronger for the contralateral paw. Choice
        left/right in their figures is the REPORT side, not the
        paw. Wheel sign is the report's direction, not which limb
        produced it.

So: they assumed contralateral representation at the level of the
insertion grid; they warned that wheel turning should not map cleanly
onto lateralized motor areas; they did not run the test. Our two
folders are that test, still unrun until someone trains a model.

19.6 What the rest of the literature says

The anatomical default in mouse and rat is contralateral.

    Corticospinal neurons in caudal and rostral forelimb areas
    (roughly MOp and an adjacent premotor patch) are the usual
    controllers of reaching and grasping. Ablation and imaging
    studies of pellet retrieval assign reach to CFA and grasp to
    RFA, both acting on the opposite forelimb. Precision isometric
    pull, a cousin of "drag this object," depends on CST integrity
    in a way that a sloppier pull does not. That is contralateral
    control of a skilled distal action.

Ipsilateral motor cortex is not silent. It is not the primary
controller either.

    A minority of CST axons do not cross, or recross, and often
    terminate more ventrally, including near motor neurons (recent
    mouse reconstructions of ipsilateral-projecting CSNs). ICMS in
    rat M1 can evoke ipsilateral joints, but those sites almost
    always also represent the same joint contralaterally; inactivate
    the opposite M1 or cut the other pyramid and the ipsilateral
    map collapses (Brus-Ramer, Carmel, Martin, J Neurosci 2009).
    Unilateral motor-cortex lesions in rat impair skilled reaching
    with the IPSILATERAL paw as well as the contralateral one,
    with incomplete recovery (Gonzalez, Gharbawie, Williams, et al.,
    EJN 2004). Skilled isometric-pull training expands the
    ipsilateral forelimb ICMS map, then the map shrinks again
    despite maintained performance (Milliken / Pruitt et al.).
    Contralaterally-projecting CSNs in mouse still fire during
    ipsilateral lever presses; some prefer ipsi, some contra, some
    both, and that mix shifts with learning (iScience 2024).

    Interhemispheric cortex-to-cortex pathways matter for sequential
    bimanual actions (eNeuro 2021). About a quarter of ModelData is
    wheel_paws == "both". That folder does not exist yet. Those
    trials are the ones for which "which hemisphere" is the wrong
    first question.

Overtraining can reduce M1's necessity. Head-fixed mice moving a
joystick become less impaired by M1 inactivation after many weeks
(Hwang, Dahlen, Hu, et al., Sci Adv 2019). The IBL mice are experts
on this wheel. A well-practiced plant-and-drag may live more in
brainstem, cerebellum, and spinal loops than a naive reach. That
does not flip the laterality of whatever cortical command remains.
It may shrink how much MOp you need, ipsi or contra.

19.7 What we think, stated as a hypothesis this pair of folders
     is built to test

A free paw spinning a Lego wheel is not a ballistic pellet reach
and not a pure isometric pull. It is hold, lift, plant, drag,
sometimes with the other paw hitchhiking. The descending command
for the acting paw should still be predominantly CONTRALATERAL,
because that is how the CST is built. Ipsilateral motor cortex
should contribute posture, axial stabilization, and some of the
bimanual or corrective component — more than in a center-out
reach, less than a 50/50 split.

Therefore:

    If that hypothesis is right, a decoder from these MOp/MOs
    cells to paw speed should work BETTER on ModelDataRight
    (contralateral pairing, 99.3%) than on ModelDataLeft
    (ipsilateral pairing, 94.2%), especially in the COUPLED
    regime, and especially in MOp rather than MOs.

    If the folders decode about equally, then either (i) much of
    the "motor" signal in this task is unlateralized vigor /
    posture / decision, or (ii) ipsilateral cortex really does
    drive this particular learned manipulation, or (iii) the
    decoder is using non-motor columns on the probe and the
    laterality of MOp is being washed out.

    If ModelDataLeft is BETTER, something in the hypothesis is
    wrong, or KS023's 14% of left-paw trials (a contralateral
    exception inside the ipsilateral folder) is doing something
    pathological to the comparison, or mouse identity is
    confounding paw (KS023 dominates Left; CSH_ZAD_026 / NR_0027
    dominate Right). Any Left-versus-Right model comparison MUST
    split or weight by mouse, or it is not a laterality test.

We have not trained that decoder. The folders make the test
possible without mixing effectors. They do not perform it.

19.8 Caveats that would make a careless reader lie

    Coverage does not "follow" the paw. The probe was already in
    left cortex. Paw use varies inside that constraint.

    Unique units do not differ by folder. Only trial weights do.

    KS023 is a contralateral island inside ModelDataLeft. A
    laterality comparison that does not isolate or drop that
    session will understate how ipsilateral the rest of Left is.

    stored kinematics still follow used_paw. Laterality of the
    LABEL is clean. Laterality of the TRACE is not, on 1,158
    Left trials and 2,454 Right trials.

    Cerebellum in the BWM is mostly RIGHT hemisphere, which is
    IPSILATERAL to the right body. If a session's second probe is
    in right cerebellum, ModelDataRight may be contralateral in
    cortex and ipsilateral in cerebellum at the same time. We did
    not split cerebellar units by hemi in this count. The 97.7%
    figure is MOp/MOs only.

    "Mostly" is 94.2% and 99.3%, not 100%. Do not write "Left is
    ipsilateral, Right is contralateral" without the percentages
    and without KS023 2019-12-10. The next section removes that
    impurity.

21. Step K — Right-camera ×2, 2D speed, speedNormalized
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Script: /Users/Stefan/Desktop/rescale_paw_speed.py
Applied to every BehaviorRes folder after LeftIpsi / RightContra
existed: Database, DatasetFiltered, both discretized copies,

paw traces mean.

21.1 Why the stored speed was in mixed units

IBL's recording-rig cameras:

    left     1280 × 1024    60 Hz
    right     640 ×  512   150 Hz

used_paw names the near camera, so left-paw x/y live on the fine
grid and right-paw x/y live on the coarse grid. 100 px/s on the
right camera is roughly twice the physical motion of 100 px/s on
the left camera. Pooling raw speed across ModelDataLeft and
ModelDataRight, or even across used_paw inside ModelData, treats
those as the same quantity. They are not.

z made it worse. z = x_near − x_far subtracts a coordinate on one
grid from a coordinate on the other. Euclidean
sqrt(vx^2+vy^2+vz^2) then mixes image-plane pixels with that
disparity. On right-paw trials vz was often the largest term,
so "speed" was mostly the stereo residual, not the reach.

There is still no millimetre calibration. The honest fixes that do
not invent one are: (1) put both cameras in left-camera pixels,
(2) keep speed in the image plane, (3) remove session-and-camera
scale without deleting within-session vigor.

21.2 What was done to every trial

    1. Right-camera ×2.
       If used_paw == "right", multiply near-camera x, y, vx, vy
       by 2. Left-paw image-plane traces are already on the 1280
       grid and are not scaled.
       z and vz are rebuilt so the disparity is
       x_near_scaled − x_far_scaled. Algebra on the stored
       (pre-scale) traces:
           left paw:  z_new = 2 z − x,   vz_new = 2 vz − vx
           right paw: z_new = x + z,     vz_new = vx + vz
       Then x_right and y_right are doubled. z is still not
       millimetres. It is now at least in the same pixel units
       as x.

    2. dlc_speed and lp_speed stay, and become 2D.
       speed = hypot(vx, vy) after the scale. vz is not in it.
       The column is not removed. This is the un-normalized
       speed a decoder can still use if it wants left-camera-
       equivalent pixels/s.

    3. speedNormalized is added.
       For each tracker, divide that trial's 2D speed by that
       session's median speed on samples whose paw_state is
       COUPLED, computed separately for used_paw left vs right
       (the near camera). Fallback if a (session, tracker, paw)
       has fewer than 5 finite coupled samples: that tracker's
       coupled median over both paws, then that tracker's
       median of all finite speeds.
       Stored as:
           dlc_speedNormalized
           lp_speedNormalized
           speedNormalized          LP if the trial has LP,
                                    else DLC
           dlc_speed_norm_ref
           lp_speed_norm_ref        the divisor, also in
                                    trials.parquet

    4. STILL / COUPLED / DECOUPLED and wheel_paws are NOT
       recomputed. Those labels were set on native-camera
       pixels (floors 140 px/s, 100 px/s). Re-thresholding
       after ×2 would have silently relabeled right-paw
       movement. speedNormalized uses the old coupled mask
       and the new 2D scaled speed. That is intentional:
       "coupled" still means what the videos were labeled
       as, and the divisor is the typical planted drag in
       the units we now store.

Do not z-score within a trial. That would set every trial's
speed std to 1 and delete vigor, which is a motor variable.
A coupled bin of speedNormalized ≈ 1 is a typical planted
drag for that session, camera, and tracker. Still stays near
0. A vigorous flick stays > 1.

fullData.py now applies the ×2 at pose extraction for any
future rebuild. enrich_and_discretize.py writes 2D speed and
speedNormalized. The patch script is what the folders on disk
actually ran.

21.3 What this does and does not fix

Fixed: left vs right camera pixel size in x, y, vx, vy, and
in 2D speed. Fixed: z no longer inside the speed scalar.
Fixed: session-and-camera scale, for anyone who uses
speedNormalized.

Not fixed: millimetres. Not fixed: used_paw vs wheel_paws
mismatches (the traces still follow used_paw; the ×2 follows
used_paw, which is the correct camera for those arrays). Not
fixed: DLC vs LP jitter (use one tracker, or the per-tracker
normalized series). Not fixed: regime thresholds still live
in native pixels.

On SWC_038 after the patch, right-paw lp_x doubled (~248 to
~496), every speed trace matched hypot(vx, vy), and coupled
speedNormalized had median 1.0 on both paws. Across ModelData,
median lp_speed_norm_ref is about 545 px/s on left-paw trials
and 769 px/s on right-paw trials: even after ×2 the typical
coupled drag is not the same physical number, which is why the
divisor is per used_paw, not one session scalar.

chosen months of pipeline earlier. On 1,158 ModelDataLeft trials,
used_paw is "right" even though wheel_paws is "left." On 2,454
ModelDataRight trials, used_paw is "left" even though wheel_paws is
"right." The label says which paw did the work; the arrays may be
the other paw. If you need matching kinematics, restrict to
used_paw == wheel_paws (6,015 Left; 4,771 Right) or re-extract that
camera's paw_r.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
21. Authors versus us
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

A. Scientific question

    Them: Where in the brain is the decision, and can we read it
          out? Movement is how the decision is reported.
    Us:   How is the report implemented as a limb action, and can
          we read THAT out of motor cortex (and friends)? The
          decision is the reason the action happens, not the thing
          we decode.

B. Movement variable

    Them: Wheel angular velocity, one signed scalar, same object
          in every lab. A proxy for the report.
    Us:   Paw kinematics (x, y in left-camera-equivalent pixels;
          z as disparity, not in speed; vx, vy, vz; 2D speed =
          hypot(vx, vy); speedNormalized = speed / session
          median coupled speed) plus a three-way regime. The
          wheel is kept to DEFINE coupling and as a baseline
          target, not as what cortex means.

C. Effector

    Them: None. The wheel has no paw. They noted that some mice use
          one paw and some use both, then decoded the wheel because
          they did not expect a simple map onto "laterally specific
          motor areas."
    Us:   used_paw (who moved, in pixels), then wheel_paws (who
          engaged the wheel), then ModelDataLeft (left only,
          mostly ipsilateral to recorded MOp/MOs) and
          ModelDataRight (right only, mostly contralateral to
          recorded MOp/MOs).

D. Time window

    Them: firstMovement − 0.2 to +1.0 s, always 60 bins of 20 ms.
          Peri-movement, ballistic habit, comparable across labs.
    Us:   stimOn to reward, variable n_bins of 20 ms, leftover
          dropped. Keeps still, keeps extra primitives, refuses
          to pretend long and short trials are time-warped twins.

E. Missing data

    Them: Extrapolate wheel if the 1.2 s window sticks out.
    Us:   NaN. Do not invent a limb.

F. Trials

    Both: bwm_include, RT 0.08–2.0 s, same freeze.
    Us additionally, in the modeling copies: pose present, reward
        only, stereo z, >= 5 MOp/MOs units, no 0% contrast,
        n_bins <= 500, then left paw or right paw in twin folders.

G. Units

    Both: the IBL three-test isolation QC frozen as
          clusters.pqt label >= 1 (median amplitude > 50 µV;
          sliding refractory-period contamination < 10% at 90%
          confidence; amplitude histogram not truncated,
          noise_cutoff < 5), spike-sorting revision 2024-05-06.
          75,708 such units in the full BWM; 13,929 of them in
          our motor-cortex-cohort Database; 10,537 in
          ModelDataLeft / ModelDataRight.
    Us:   admission by motor cortex count; columns still include
          the rest of the probe. We did not add isolation-distance,
          L-ratio, or a private SNR cut on top of theirs. We did
          not pick the hemisphere to match the paw; the grid
          already had.

H. Motor primitives

    Them: none.
    Us:   still / coupled / decoupled as a hypothesis about
          control modes that compose a non-ballistic action.

I. What success looks like

    Them: High decoding of choice across many regions; wheel
          decoding as a movement control analysis.
    Us:   A model that predicts paw speed, that does better in
          coupled than a wheel model does on decoupled, that
          changes its latent state at regime transitions, that
          does not get that performance by memorizing one mouse,
          and that, if contralateral CST control is real for this
          movement, does better on ModelDataRight than on
          ModelDataLeft.

22. Limitations you should not paper over
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

    Stored kinematics follow used_paw, not always wheel_paws
        (1,158 mismatched trials in ModelDataLeft; 2,454 in
        ModelDataRight).

    Stereo z is disparity, not millimetres, even after the
        right-camera ×2. Speed is 2D hypot(vx, vy) in left-
        camera-equivalent pixels/s and does not include z.
        speedNormalized removes session-and-camera scale
        (median coupled speed per tracker and used_paw) without
        killing within-session vigor. It is still not millimetres.
        Cross-session pooling of raw dlc_speed / lp_speed is less
        wrong than before the ×2, and still worse than using
        speedNormalized.

    No force, no EMG, no digits. Coupled is "looks planted and the
        wheel moved," not "measured contact force."

    The three-regime machine is a heuristic. Thresholds were set
        from videos of a few mice. PawStrats exists so you can
        fight us.

    97.7% of motor units are left hemisphere. ModelDataLeft is
        therefore mostly ipsilateral (94.2% of motor unit-trials).
        ModelDataRight is mostly contralateral (99.3%). That is
        the recording grid, not a paw-matched implant. KS023 is a
        contralateral island inside Left. Comparing the two
        folders without a mouse split confounds paw with which
        mice like which paw.

    n_bins still varies (8–494 Left, 8–484 Right). That is the
        behavior. A model must handle it (masking, recurrence, set
        models, per-bin losses). Do not warp.

    n_units varies by session (41–524). Unit 17 is not a common
        neuron across mice.

    KS023 is 14% of ModelDataLeft. CSH_ZAD_026 is 12% of
        ModelDataRight. Pooled numbers without a mouse split are
        those mice's numbers.

    "Both" was discarded rather than studied. Bimanual control is
        a quarter of ModelData and possibly the most interesting
        motor object in the project. Left and Right are
        simplifications for a first laterality test, not a claim
        that mice are unimanual.

    We have not, in building this folder, trained the decoder.
        Everything above is a claim about what the dataset MAKES
        TESTABLE. It is not a result.

25. The last paragraph
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The Brain-Wide Map paper gave the field a standardized decision,
a standardized wheel, and a map of where that decision can be read
out. That is a genuine achievement, and we stood on it: same mice,
same trials, same units, same 20 ms bin.

What they treated as a convenience — the wheel as movement — is,
for motor cortex, a category error. Cortex does not innervate the
wheel. It generates hold, free movement, and object-engaged drag,
in some order, with one paw or two, for a variable amount of real
time. Those are intermediate primitives that compose a report which
only LOOKS like a single left-or-right answer in the experimenter's
log.

They knew the paws were not interchangeable. They wrote that some
mice use one paw and some use both, that the turn is not ballistic,
and that one should not expect a simple map onto laterally specific
motor areas. Then they decoded the wheel. We split the effectors
instead.

ModelDataLeft and ModelDataRight are the datasets you get if you
take that sentence seriously: video not just encoder, paw not just
wheel, regimes not just speed, stimulus-to-reward not a 1.2 s
cookie, one effector not a mixture, variable length not a warp,
and laterality not an average. Left is the ipsilateral folder,
because IBL put almost all motor cortex in the left hemisphere and
we kept left-paw work. Right is the contralateral folder, same
cells, the other paw. Whether that anatomical default actually
governs a learned wheel spin is the test those two folders exist
to run.

This file is not that result. It is why the folders are shaped so
that the result, when someone trains the model, is about a limb,
a hemisphere, and not a proxy.

End of write-up.
